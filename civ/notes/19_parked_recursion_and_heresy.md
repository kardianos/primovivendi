## 1. Recursive justification

### The idea

From the author's letter to Brad (draft, 2026-11-13):

> When we discover something that works, like crop rotation, or concrete, we then get to ask for the justification: why did it work? We might try out different justifications: local spirits, the roots themselves, the different plants decompose differently. But once you land on a justification that can generalize eg bacteria in the roots, and then further justification, specific chemicals that get fixed by those bacteria, you can learn to directly apply any material that has those properties to the soil, and it works.

Each explanation that holds under testing becomes a new claim, and its cause can be sought in turn. Each answer applies more widely than the one before it, and the answers compound.

### What the book has now

- **02:78**, sheet line 77, eng 1.4: justification is having a reason to think a claim corresponds; a claim is justified when it has been tested against reality and holds up; independent tests that converge make it stronger. One level only.
- **02:102**, the clover rotation: the practice is justified by the grain, the spirit account is not, and later testing justified the bacteria. The passage stops there. It does not ask why the bacteria work or what that answer lets a farmer do.
- **02:111** and **02:123**, incomplete models: the successor model "explains *why* the earlier model worked inside its boundary," and the chain from Kepler to Newton to Einstein is "cumulative truth-seeking." This is the recursion in practice (Newton explains Kepler's laws; Einstein explains why Newton's hold at ordinary speeds), but the book does not name it.
- **Note 14, section 7**: proxies can be chained, and proxies are learned by testing them. Section 13 lists as open "How the standard of justification, testing against Reality, is itself justified." That is a different recursion (the regress of reasons), not the one above.

### Terms to settle

- In philosophy, "recursive justification" reads as the regress problem: every reason needs a reason, so the chain runs forever, circles, or stops at a foundation (Aristotle, *Posterior Analytics* I.3). Plantinga's answer to it is "properly basic" beliefs. A reader of Plantinga will likely take the phrase that way.
- What the letter describes is closer to asking for the explanation again at each level. In the book's vocabulary (02:78, 02:102), each explanation is a claim that must be justified by its own tests. Aristotle's distinction between knowing *that* and knowing *why* (*Posterior Analytics* I.13) is an old name for the step.
- Possible wording: "recursive explanation," or "each justified explanation is itself a claim whose cause can be sought."

### Points for the letter

- Plantinga's conditions for warrant ask about the knower: faculties working properly, in a suitable environment, by a design plan aimed at truth. Put to crop rotation, they examine the farmer, while the farmer's question is about the soil. That is why they get nowhere with it.
- Plantinga's proper functionalism is usually classed as a relative of reliabilism. Brad may answer "reliabilism is closest" with that.
- The history ran from both ends (from memory, to verify): the nitrogen need was shown first (Boussingault, 1830s; Lawes and Gilbert at Rothamsted, 1840s and 1850s), and the bacteria later (Hellriegel and Wilfarth, 1886 to 1888). The two lines met. The letter tells the order as bacteria, then chemicals.
- Small fixes in the draft: "'Empiricism' and 'Logical Positivism' are off the mark"; "asking those questions or following those methods."

### Draft for the book, after 02:102

> The same question can then be asked of the new explanation. What the bacteria leave in the soil is nitrogen in a form a plant can take up, and anything that supplies that form does the clover's work: manure, guano, or ammonia made from the air in a factory. The deeper explanation also marks where the old practice fails. Clover sown in soil that lacks the right bacteria fixes little nitrogen, and farmers learned to coat the seed with the bacteria before sowing. Each explanation that holds under testing becomes a claim whose own cause can be sought, and each answer applies more widely than the one before it.

To verify from primary sources before use: Hellriegel and Wilfarth on nodule bacteria (1886 to 1888); Nobbe and Hiltner's "Nitragin" seed inoculant (patented 1896); Haber's ammonia synthesis (1909) and the BASF plant at Oppau (1913); Boussingault and Lawes and Gilbert on nitrogen. Sheet line 77 and eng 1.4 might take one sentence on the same point.

## 2. Sacred explanations, heresy, and who may think freely

### The idea, in the author's words (2026-11-13)

> we can talk about civilizations which subsitute God or gods to positive aspects, but risk genuine progress with herasy charges. -- Maybe humanity needs more ridigity. Maybe we need to only allow free thinking in people who can freely think or think in terms of 2nd or 3rd order consequences. I don't know.

Two threads:

1. A civilization credits God or gods with the things that work (a working practice, a moral order, a law). Then, when someone tests the explanation, the charge of heresy can stop the testing and with it genuine progress.
2. A tentative counter-thought: perhaps humanity needs more rigidity, and free thinking should be allowed only to people who can think freely, or who reason about second- and third-order consequences. The author marked this "I don't know."

### Link to section 1

The two ideas share one shape. When the explanation attached to a working practice is made sacred, asking why it works becomes heresy, and the recursion in section 1 stops at its first level. 02:102 already has this in miniature: the traditionalist who holds the spirit account as literal truth "prevents the village from ever understanding soil chemistry," while the reformer who drops the practice starves the village.

### Passages that bear on it

- **02:102**: keep the working practice; test its explanation on its own evidence. One possible line for the rigidity thought is that the rigidity belongs to the practice and the freedom to the explanation. This is an observation for later, not a decision.
- **02:68**: "Reasonable people will study and debate long-term chains of consequences," with the example of a policy that helps in the first month and harms in the twentieth year. This is the second- and third-order idea already in the book.
- **02:64**: Lysenko, a state orthodoxy that imprisoned the geneticists who tested it.
- **12:81**: the Two-Books Doctrine, which the book credits with incubating the Scientific Revolution. Heresy charges arose inside the same order, so the section would need to say how both hold.
- **12:124** to **12:132**: apostasy and blasphemy law, placed under Principle 1 because a revealed claim must still be tested.
- **13:162** and **13:168**: blasphemy shields; one rule of free speech for every citizen.
- **14:54** to **14:66**, Tool 4: free speech as "a civilization's primary mechanism for detecting and correcting error," grounded in Principles 1 and 2.

### Questions to settle

- Whether the rigidity thought fits Tool 4 and Principle 2. If every magistrate is limited and fallible, who decides which people can think freely, and what checks that judgment?
- Whether "free thinking" here means thought, speech, teaching, or the authority to change a practice. These have different scopes, and Principle 5 may separate them.
- Which historical cases to use, from primary sources. Candidates: Galileo (1616 and 1633), Lysenko (already in chapter 2), and cases where a sacred explanation did not stop testing.
- Where it belongs: chapter 2 (practice and explanation), chapter 12 (revealed claims), or chapter 14 (free speech).
