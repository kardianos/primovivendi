# From `ccp_eng.md` and `ccp_derive.md`, kept at their removal

Recorded 2026-11-15. The author removed `ccp_eng.md` and `ccp_derive.md`. Both restated the sheet and the book, and both had drifted from them. This note keeps what neither the sheet nor the book carries, for later evaluation. Nothing here has been applied.

Both files can still be read from git: `git show c19de61:civ/ccp_eng.md` and `git show c19de61:civ/ccp_derive.md`. In this note, "eng" and "derive" line numbers refer to that commit, and chapter line numbers refer to the chapters on this date.

## 1. The systems frame (eng)

Eng treated the five principles and the virtues as the steps of an engineering analysis of a system. The preface (eng:9–16):

> In engineering, a system is analyzed by identifying:
>
> 1. The initial conditions of the components.
> 2. The constraints of the environment in which the system operates.
> 3. The material and operational properties of the components.
> 4. The objective function the system must satisfy to avoid termination.
> 5. The resource-allocation and conflict-resolution protocols required to keep the components working together.
> 6. The behavioral specifications required of each individual component so that the entire system continues to function over time.

The conclusion (eng:719–734) gave each step its content:

| Step of the analysis | Content |
|---|---|
| Initial conditions | The newborn without tools; technology; civilization as the prerequisite technology |
| Environment | Principle 1 |
| Components | Principle 2 |
| Objective function | Principle 3 |
| Resource allocation and threat response | Principle 4 |
| Conflict protocol among many agents | Principle 5 |
| Behavior of each component | The seven virtues |

The book's chapter titles echo parts of this: "The Human Builder" (chapter 4), "The Minimum Standard of the Good" (chapter 6), and "The Resource-Allocation Problem of Finite Builders" (08:3). The book never states the whole map, and the phrase "objective function" appears nowhere in it. The phrase comes from mid-twentieth-century operations research and may be absent from the 1850–1950 corpus. Check it with the ngram tool before use.

The preface also stated two rules of method (eng:18). "Every word in this document is used in its literal sense." Each opposing view "is evaluated by testing what happens in the physical world when [its] proposals are executed."

Eng gave every term four parts: Derivation, Detractors and Failure Modes, Operational Tolerances, and Excluded Parameters. The sheet keeps three: Why it is important, Range of views, and What it cannot accept. The extra part named each opposing view and showed how it fails in practice. Most of those views also appear in the sheet's "cannot accept" lines or in the book's prose.

**Question:** whether chapter 1 should state the map in one paragraph, near "The Architecture in Full" (01:73).

## 2. Will without method, and method without will (eng)

The book defines will and method (01:44–49). It does not name the two ways the pair fails. Eng 6.2 (eng:521–524):

> Both components are strictly necessary:
>
> * **Will without Method:** A population that desires to survive and prosper, but has lost or destroyed the technical and social methods required to farm, manufacture, and govern under symmetrical law, will fail through incompetence and disorder.
> * **Method without Will:** A population that possesses advanced libraries, industrial factories, and written legal codes, but loses the will to work, enforce its laws, defend its borders, and have children, will cease to exist within a few generations. Its buildings and books will either deteriorate or be taken over by another population that possesses the will to continue.

Eng 6.3 (eng:527) set civilization beside a structure that stands by itself:

> A stone bridge remains standing by passive mechanical equilibrium even when no human is thinking about it; a civilization does not.

The bridge sentence pulls against the new opening of chapter 1, "Nothing we build keeps itself" (01:7). The bridge stands for a time without attention, but it does not keep itself. If used, the sentence needs a time scale, or a different contrast.

**Question:** whether the two failures belong in chapter 1 after 01:49, or in chapter 11 with the mechanisms of decline.

## 3. The starting premise (derive)

Derive §1 (derive:9) named one assumed priority and defended it:

> We will allow ourselves only one starting priority: **we want to build things that work in the physical world, and we want human beings to survive and continue doing so from the past, through the present, into the future.** We do not need to apologize for assuming this priority, because any person who argues against it is only able to formulate his argument by eating food, sitting in a heated building, and using a language and a computer network built by people who held this priority. A system of thought that cannot keep its operators alive and cannot build tools that work eliminates itself from the world.

The nearest passage in the book is 06:9. It says physical description alone does not compel anyone to care whether he lives, so "Every moral and political system therefore begins by affirming a standard of goodness." The last sentence of the quote is already in the book at 06:22 ("eliminates itself from reality"). The book does not say at the start which priority it assumes. It also does not give derive's defense, that the critic of the priority is kept alive by people who hold it.

Points for evaluation:

- The defense shows that the critic acts on the priority. It does not show that the priority is correct. A reader trained in logic may call it a tu quoque.
- "We do not need to apologize" answers an objection before anyone raises it. That cuts against the positive, not comparative, rule.
- The new chapter 1 opening says reality answers atheists and people of faith in the same way. A stated starting priority would sit beside that claim and should not narrow it.

**Question:** whether chapter 1 or 06:9 should state the assumed priority, and with which defense, if any.

## 6. Derive's order of presentation

Derive carried one airplane from start to finish. It brought in each virtue at the step that called for it:

1. P1, then Virtue 1 (§2)
2. P2, then Virtue 5 (§3)
3. P3, then Virtue 3 (§4)
4. Virtue 4, from two hangars (§5)
5. Virtue 2, from the shared shop, then P4 (§6)
6. P5 (§7)
7. Virtues 6 and 7, from the eighty-year horizon (§8)

The book follows much the same order across chapters 2 to 10, with a different running example in each chapter. This is recorded as a choice of presentation only.
