## Life, and the new individual

Life began eons ago. Conception begins a new individual. It does not begin life.

The question often put by people arguing against abortion is "When does life begin?" The answers then offered are that life begins at conception, or that life begins when the child is wanted. Both answers treat a moment as the start of life. Asked that way, the question assumes that life needs a spirit, an essence, or some other extra-material thing, put into the organism at that moment. That assumption is the weak part of the framing.

The more useful framing keeps the two things apart. Life is the continuous life of living things, already ancient at the moment a particular child is conceived. The new fact at conception is the individual.

## Two orientations

People who support abortion, and people who obtain abortions, are alive. The distinction is the orientation of the life they are living.

One orientation takes experience as the goal. The primary motive is experience, often first named as pleasure. The person still wants to experience certain things, and will not change his life in order to build. Under that orientation a child is a hindrance. Abortion then follows as a way to keep the experiences.

The other orientation takes building and keeping as the goal. The child is then something the person is actively building and keeping, as part of life. The mindset changes with the goal.

## Readiness, money, and comfort

"I am not ready" and "I cannot afford a child" are, in general, immature claims. They are not a rule without exception. Hardship is real. An extra bedroom would be welcome. More food would be welcome. Being able to pay a babysitter sometimes would be welcome.

Those wants are not the same thing as an inability to keep a child. The author lived for many years on oatmeal, butter, and eggs, which are cheap, in years when he had very little money. What is often being refused is a restructuring of life: a change the person does not want, because he still wants to experience certain things. The admission is about values. The value system is experience. Building is set aside when it would cost him the experiences he still wants.

People in that orientation want to stay in comfort and then add a child as a luxury item. The civilizational orientation is different. A family is being built. A life is being built. Something is being built out of nothing, and the child is part of that life. Building past the comfort one already has, into something greater, matters more than doing the same work from the comfort of a luxury liner. The comparison is exaggerated for effect. The difference in importance is real.

## Born into civilization, and taught

A person is born into civilization. The orientation toward building and keeping is taught. It does not arise on its own. It has to be passed down, and the passing down is mostly the work of the family.

## A fundamental aspect of life

Children are a fundamental aspect of life. They are not the foundation on which everything else is built, as if the rest of life were arranged around them and had no other standing.

They belong to adult life as much as childhood belongs to the life of a child. Adults and children require one another. There are no children without adults, and there are no adults without children. An adult needs an adult life, and an adult needs children. Cutting out either one breaks the cycle.

## What civilization is for

Valuing life, and orienting toward life that continues and is kept and built, is an is-ought problem. It is at the same time a foundational problem and a civilizational problem. The civilization has to orient toward it. That orientation is what civilization is for.

Civilization is for keeping life and for building life up. It is for creating something new, for expanding, and for realizing what people can do. Children are included in that work. Over time the work requires children. A person is severely limited. He cannot keep or build anything past a definite date, the end of his own life. No single life carries the civilization forward by itself.

## Birth, the debt, and replacement

A civilization that cannot keep its birth rate up has a problem in its own premise. The failure is a civilizational debt: the continuation that the living are not providing.

Covering that debt by migration or immigration does not repair the premise. It states that this civilization cannot continue itself. The proposal is often described as taking other people into the civilization. What follows from a failure at that premise is the replacement of the civilization. The replacement is at the level of the premise, not a change of membership inside an unchanged civilization.

7. Children are a fundamental aspect of life. They are not the foundation of everything else. Adults need an adult life, and adults need children. Adults and children require one another. Cutting out either side breaks the cycle.
