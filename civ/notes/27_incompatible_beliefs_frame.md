## The purpose of this material

This was recorded 2026-11-19 at the author's direction, and corrected the same day.

**The intended reader.** The book is written for a reader who takes what he believes seriously and has no wish to speak heresy against his own creed. He works step by step through what follows from living in a shared reality with people who believe otherwise. The book is not designed for the reader who holds all beliefs to be one soft mixture. That reader gets notice only: the book does not proceed that way. Nor does it ask the earnest reader to treat his creed as one more strand of such a mixture.

Every piece below is judged by that purpose:
- **The introduction** must tell the earnest reader that the book takes his belief seriously, and that following its argument does not require him to speak against his creed. It must also tell the other reader, in passing, that the book does not treat all gods as the same. Draft 1 does the second in two sentences: "That reading takes none of them at its word" and "This book takes each faith and philosophy at its word instead." It does the first only by implication; a proposed sentence is marked in Draft 1.
- **The middle** must show it in practice. It quotes each creed from its own texts, judges each by its own word, and asks of it only what follows from sharing one reality and one law.
- **The conclusion** must restate it.

## The idea, as the author gave it

1. **People hold beliefs that are mutually incompatible.**
2. **There are two ways to read the disagreement.**
   - The first treats all beliefs as pointing to one nebulous good and counts the differences as small. That reading is in fact a denial of all of them, in favour of something else, perhaps nihilism.
   - The second takes each seriously. It accepts that not all can be correct. Where people try to live together, they still need principles by which to decide how to act, as a society and as a civilization.
3. **Not every society is compatible with every other.** The limits some creeds set will be incompatible with others, for example Islam's limits for non-Muslims.
4. **The book does not set out to diminish any thought.** It takes each seriously and at its word.
5. **"Freedom of religion" worked when the religions were strains of Christianity.** It may not work across every religion and philosophy. An Islamic country might be a reasonable trading partner without being compatible with an unbounded freedom of religion.
6. **The aim is not sacrilegious.** The book is a rubric and strategy for civilization.

## The strategy

The book's subject is the principles of civilization, not freedom of religion. The material enters the book in three places, and only as far as the book's own argument needs it:
- **The introduction** says that beliefs contradict one another and that the book takes each at its word.
- **Chapter 9** states, as a corollary of scope and symmetry, what people of different creeds must grant one another to live under one law.
- **The conclusion** turns that into where a people gets along with others, and how.

### The six points

The author agreed to these six points on 2026-11-19.

1. **The subject is one civil order, not freedom of religion.** The material belongs to the book only as an application of Principle 5: how scope and symmetry settle which people of different creeds can share one law. Freedom of religion is one historical arrangement for that, not the subject. The section in chapter 9 is titled "Many Creeds Under One Law", and the conclusion keeps "Where We Get Along". Both stay short, as corollaries of scope rather than a treatise on religious liberty, and that keeps the book out of First Amendment doctrine and its edge cases.
2. **The conditions are the civilization's rubric, not a claimant's list of rights.** They are questions a people asks in judging whether a creed's followers can share its civil order. They are not entitlements any group can invoke against that people. The tactic of claiming religious freedom as a demand on the community then has nothing to grip.
3. **Freedom of belief stays a negative boundary,** as chapter 9 §4.1 already describes classical rights. It protects persons: belief is not a legal position, so a man may not be punished for it, kept from leaving it, or barred from arguing for it. It gives no claim on public space, public money, or public honour.
4. **The devil beside the manger is the outcome counterfeit,** which chapter 9 treats as a form of value symmetry.
   - **What procedural symmetry protects.** It holds the rule equal for persons in the same position. A Satanist's person, speech, exit, and covenants are protected exactly as a Christian's are.
   - **What the manger is.** A town's nativity scene is the town speaking in its own space, from its own inheritance. Principles 2 and 4 allow it, because people are particular and what they keep is self-anchored and tiered.
   - **What the demand asks.** "If you show a manger you must show my devil" asks for equal honour, not equal procedure.
   - **The terms.** In chapter 9's terms it is outcome symmetry, "a form of value symmetry" (Counterfeits 1 and 2). It fails the chapter's three tests: the town's own inheritance pays, the claimant judges, and the measure is the gap between places, not any act.
5. **The rule holds both ways.** If a town opens a forum to all private displays, one rule must govern every display, because that is procedure. The town is not obliged to open one.
   - **The parallel in law.** United States law draws roughly the same line, between the government's own speech and a forum it opens to the public.
   - **The case to check.** *Pleasant Grove City v. Summum* (2009) is recalled as holding that permanent monuments in a public park are the government's own speech. Verify before citing.
6. **Taking a group at its word works here too.** If a group's own statements say it does not worship a devil, and uses the imagery as protest, then it is by its own word a political movement in religious dress. The rubric judges it as one.
   - **The Satanic Temple.** It is recalled as saying this of itself. Check its own published statements before relying on that.
   - **Not named in the book.** Naming it gives it a platform and invites the distraction. At most one unnamed sentence illustrates the counterfeit.
   - **The author's expansion.** If it is genuine devil worship, it contradicts Principle 3 ("Goodness must include human life continuing, and that life kept and built"), and is not fit for society. It fails before the swap is asked.

## Draft 1: introduction (Chapter 1, after 1:17)

> Reality is shared, but beliefs are not. People hold beliefs about God, the soul, and the good that contradict one another. One may treat them all as dim views of a single vague good, and count the differences as small. That reading takes none of them at its word. It sets each aside for a belief that none of their followers holds, and carried to its end it affirms nothing. This book takes each faith and philosophy at its word instead. **[proposed]** A reader need not speak against his own creed to follow its argument. It asks only what follows from living in one reality with people who believe otherwise. **[end proposed]** Where two contradict each other, both cannot be correct. Their followers must still live beside one another, and to do so they need principles by which a society, and a civilization, decides how to act. Not every creed can share one civil order with every other, though each may keep a civilization of its own. Chapter 9 sets out what people of different creeds must grant one another to live under one law. This book does not set out to diminish any faith. It is a rubric and a strategy for civilization: a way for any people to judge what will keep and build their common life, and with whom they can build it.

## Draft 2: "Many Creeds Under One Law" (Chapter 9, end of §3)

> #### Many Creeds Under One Law
>
> A civil order has one scope, and people of many creeds live inside it. Layer C showed that a law drawn by confession and a law drawn by citizenship do not make one polity. The question here is narrower: what must the followers of different creeds grant one another to live under one law? The answer is the procedural symmetry of this section, applied to creeds.
>
> **The kind of symmetry.** It is procedural symmetry, as defined at the head of this section. Interchange the labels of two parties in the same position within the same scope, and the procedure does not change. Its working form is the label-swap test of Chapter 14. Would the creed accept, applied to itself, the rule it applies to others in the same position?
>
> **The test belongs to the civilization, not to the claimant.** These are questions a people asks in judging whether a creed's followers can share its civil order. They are not rights that any group can claim against that people.
>
> **What freedom of belief protects.** It is a negative boundary, of the kind Section 4 describes, and it protects persons. A man's belief is not a legal position. He may not be punished for holding it, kept from leaving it, or barred from arguing for it. The boundary gives no claim on public space, public money, or public honour.
>
> **A threshold comes first: Principle 3.** A creed whose good is the destruction of human life, or that worships the destroyer, contradicts Principle 3. It is not fit for society, and the question of sharing one civil order does not arise.
>
> **The four swaps.** A creed that passes the threshold can share one law with others only if its followers meet four conditions.
> - **How they are judged.** By their own texts, by the authorities they obey, and by what they do, in power as well as in a minority.
> - **Why these four.** Each is the swap applied where a creed most often claims a one-way freedom.
>
> 1. **Exit: entry and exit.** A creed that receives converts must let its own members leave, without penalty of law and without violence or threat. It may stop counting a man a member. It may not touch his person, his property, his marriage, or his children. Exit comes first, because a man who cannot leave a creed is held in it (Principle 1).
> 2. **Criticism: preaching and being criticized.** A creed that calls other creeds false must hear itself called false. Its followers accept offence, blasphemy included, without physical retaliation of any kind. They also do not punish the offender through civil life (Principles 1 and 2).
> 3. **Covenants: insider and outsider.** The followers keep faith with outsiders as with insiders.
>    - **Consent.** Their customs bind members only by consent.
>    - **No reprieve.** The customs never grant a reprieve from the civil law. Its duties, such as registering a marriage, remain the followers' duties.
>    - **No outside authority.** The followers obey no authority outside the polity in civil matters.
>
>    Principles 3 and 4 support this condition.
> 4. **The same in power: minority and majority.** While few, the creed's followers live under a law they do not command. When they are many, they must give the same protection to the few (Principle 1).
>
> **What the swap does not require.** It holds the rule equal for persons, not the honour given to creeds.
> - **The town's own display.** A town's nativity scene is the town speaking in its own space, from its own inheritance, as Principles 2 and 4 allow.
> - **The demand for equal place.** A demand that a devil stand beside the manger asks for an equal place, not an equal procedure. It is the outcome counterfeit above, a form of value symmetry. The claimant judges, the town's own inheritance pays, and the measure is the gap between places, not any act.
> - **An open forum.** Where a town opens a forum to every private display, one rule must govern every display, because that is procedure. The town is not obliged to open one.
>
> **Each creed is taken at its word.**
> - **A movement in religious dress.** A group whose own statements say it worships no devil, and uses the image as protest, is a political movement in religious dress. It is judged as one.
> - **Genuine devil worship.** A group that does worship a devil fails the threshold of Principle 3.
>
> **Three relations follow, in the tiers of Principle 4:**
> 1. **fellow citizens,** under one law;
> 2. **neighbours,** each keeping a civilization of its own, who trade and keep treaties where verification operates (Section 5);
> 3. **enemies,** of any creed, who use force.

## Draft 3: conclusion (Chapter 14, new §3 "Where We Get Along")

> ### 3. Where We Get Along
>
> Reality is shared, but beliefs are not. One creed teaches that a man is saved by grace through faith. Another teaches that he reaches the Garden by belief and righteous deeds, under the will of God. A third teaches that the goal is the extinguishing of craving, and with it of rebirth. These are not three wordings of one belief, and this book has taken each at its word, from its own texts.
>
> People who disagree about the next world must still share roads, markets, courts, and borders in this one. Chapter 9 gave the test for living under one law: the swap of procedural symmetry. It is asked at four points: at entry and exit, in speech, in promises, and in power. It is a test a people applies, not a right any group can claim against it. A creed that sets itself against human life itself fails before the swap is asked (Principle 3).
>
> The answers sort every relation into three tiers.
> 1. **Fellow citizens.** With the followers of every creed that passes the swap, a people shares one law, one court, and one army. Each citizen may hold his belief, leave it, and argue it in public.
> 2. **Neighbours.** With a civilization whose law is drawn by confession, a people trades, keeps treaties where they can be verified, and lives at peace. It grants that law no jurisdiction inside its own borders, and it does not pretend the two orders are one.
> 3. **Enemies.** Against whoever uses force, of any creed, a people defends itself.
>
> The tiers govern ordinary conduct as well as policy.
> - **A citizen** deals truthfully and keeps his word with men of every creed, because the covenant swap binds him as it binds them.
> - **A household** joins associations and makes partnerships with people whose creed teaches them to keep faith with outsiders.
> - **A town** protects the apostate, the critic, and the open-air preacher alike, and grants no creed's customs a court, a tax, a penalty, or a reprieve from its law. It may keep its own nativity scene in its own square. It owes no claimant an equal place beside it.
>
> This book does not set out to diminish any faith or philosophy, and it is not a theology. It is a rubric and a strategy for civilization: a way for any people to judge what will keep and build their common life, and with whom they can build it.

### A.1 The conditions of freedom of belief: the earlier draft (formerly planned for Chapter 9 §4.2)

This is the rework of 2026-11-19, after the author's comments (recorded below the draft).

> #### 2. Freedom of Belief and Its Conditions
>
> Freedom of conscience is a classical constitutional right of the kind just described. It is scoped, negative, and procedural. Before the court, belief is not a legal position. Two citizens of different creeds who stand in the same position receive the same rules of contract, property, and evidence.
>
> The conditions below are not conditions of civilization as such.
> - **A civilization without freedom of religion can still endure.** It can live, keep, and build under a law drawn by confession. Islamic civilization did so for centuries, within the bounds of its scope (Layer C above; Chapter 12).
> - **These are the conditions of freedom of religion.** A civilization that espouses it must meet them. They are the terms on which people of different creeds live under one law, each free to believe, to leave, and to argue.
> - **A creed whose followers cannot meet them can keep a civilization of its own.** It cannot share this one.
>
> **The test is procedural symmetry.**
> - **The kind of symmetry.** The symmetry meant is the procedural symmetry of Principle 5, defined in Section 3 above. Interchange the labels of two parties who stand in the same position within the same scope, and the procedure does not change. It holds the rule equal, not the outcome. It does not require that every creed be equally believed, honoured, or successful. That demand is the counterfeit of value symmetry (Section 3).
> - **The working form.** It is the label-swap test of Chapter 14. Exchange the labels of the two parties, and ask whether the creed would accept, applied to itself, the rule it applies to others.
> - **Why a shared right needs it.** Freedom of belief is held in common. Each citizen enjoys it only while the creeds around him keep it toward others. A creed that claims a freedom for itself and denies it to others in the same position fails the swap. It does not share the right; it uses the right until it no longer needs it.
> - **Locke's version.** John Locke set this bound in *A Letter Concerning Toleration* (1689). He gave no right to toleration to those "that will not own and teach the duty of tolerating all men in matters of mere religion."
> - **Why these four conditions.** They are the swap applied where a creed most often claims a one-way freedom:
>   - in entering and leaving;
>   - in speech;
>   - in promises;
>   - in power.
> - **What is judged.** Each condition is judged by the creed's own texts, by the authorities its followers obey, and by what its followers do, in power as well as in a minority. What its spokesmen say is not the test.
>
> 1. **Exit.**
>    - **The condition.** A follower may leave the creed, and be known to have left, without penalty. That means no penalty of law, and no violence or threat from the creed's authorities or followers. The creed may cease to count him a member. It may not touch his person, his property, his marriage, or his children.
>    - **The swap: entry and exit.** A creed that receives converts from other creeds asks those creeds to let their members leave. Interchange the labels, and it must let its own members leave for them. A creed that welcomes converts and punishes apostates applies one rule to the man who comes and another to the man who goes. Yet both men make the same choice.
>    - **Why it comes first.** Every other freedom of belief depends on it. A man who cannot leave a creed is not free to believe it; he is held in it.
>    - **The supporting principle.** Principle 1. A belief held true must stay open to correction by reality, and a man who may not change his mind cannot correct it.
> 2. **Criticism.**
>    - **The condition.** The creed's followers accept offence to what they hold most holy, blasphemy included, without physical retaliation of any kind.
>      - **No punishment through civil life.** They do not punish the offender through civil life: by his work, his trade, his lodging, his schooling, or any office they hold over him.
>      - **No call to punish.** The creed's authorities call for no such punishment.
>      - **Objects as well as words.** The same holds for any lawful act done to an object they hold holy, by a man who owns it.
>    - **The swap: preaching and being criticized.** A creed that preaches asks others to hear that their beliefs are false, and its own scriptures say so of other creeds. Interchange the labels, and it must hear the same said of itself, in whatever words the speaker chooses. A creed that may call other creeds false, but may not be called false itself, claims a one-way freedom of speech.
>    - **Why the law is not the only measure.** A creed can enforce silence without any statute: through threats, through crowds, and through punishment in civil life. Where a cartoonist, a teacher, or a man who burns a book he owns must live under guard, the critic has been silenced as surely as by law.
>    - **The test.** If a creed's followers cannot accept verbal criticism of what they hold most holy, they cannot live with others under freedom of religion.
>    - **The supporting principles.**
>      - **Principle 1.** Claims are tested in the open.
>      - **Principle 2.** No limited human being, office, or text is exempt from error in civil matters.
> 3. **Covenants, not jurisdiction.**
>    - **The condition.** A creed may keep its own customs, vows, and rites. Its followers may hold marriage to be a sacrament. Where the civil law requires a marriage to be registered with the state, registering it remains their duty, whatever the marriage means to them. What the condition forbids is a creed's customs acting as a law of their own, binding where they should only be agreed to.
>    - **The swap, three times.**
>      - **Insider and outsider.** Promises made to those outside the creed bind as promises made inside it. Truth-telling and promise-keeping owed only inside a creed fail the swap, and make trust across it impossible (Chapter 12, on boundary speech). Locke called "promises, covenants, and oaths" "the bonds of human society."
>      - **Creed and civil law.** The creed asks the civil law to bind it only by common rule and by consent. Interchange the labels, and its customs bind its members only by consent.
>      - **Creed and rival.** The creed would not accept a rival creed's followers obeying an outside authority against it. Interchange the labels, and its own followers obey no authority outside the polity in civil matters.
>    - **Customs bind members only by consent.** A covenant is entered freely and left freely, under the exit condition. It is enforced by no penalty beyond what the civil law allows for any agreement, and it claims no power over a member's civil status.
>      - **Disputes.** Members may settle disputes among themselves by agreement, as any citizens may. Such a settlement stands or falls under the civil law like any other agreement.
>      - **No reprieve.** Nothing in the creed grants a reprieve from the civil law.
>      - **The test.** The test is not what a body calls itself, whether court, council, or mediation. It is whether anyone is held by the creed's customs against his will, or beyond what the civil law would hold him to.
>      - **The case.** The independent review of sharia councils in England and Wales (Home Office, 2018) found that the councils "claim no binding legal authority". They "do in fact act in a decision-making capacity when dealing with Islamic divorce", and "nearly all people using sharia councils, are women". The review also found that "a significant number of Muslim couples fail to civilly register their religious marriages". A woman in such a marriage can leave it only on the council's terms. That is jurisdiction, not covenant.
>    - **No outside authority.** Locke's example was the man who professes himself "a faithful subject" while "bound to yield blind obedience to the Mufti of Constantinople." Such a covenant plants, in Locke's words, "a foreign jurisdiction" inside the polity.
>    - **The supporting principles.**
>      - **Principle 3.** Life kept and built across generations rests on promises that hold.
>      - **Principle 4.** A covenant that binds men to an outside authority enlists them, in Locke's phrase, "as it were, for soldiers against his own Government."
> 4. **The same in power.**
>    - **The condition.** The creed's texts and authorities do not require that, once its followers hold a majority or power, its customs become a law binding everyone.
>    - **The swap: minority and majority.** While few, the creed's followers live under the protection of a law they do not command. Interchange the positions, and they must give the same protection to the few when they are many. Locke described the creed that fails this swap: its followers "only ask leave to be tolerated by the magistrate so long until they find themselves strong enough to effect it."
>    - **The evidence.** The evidence is the creed's own texts and authorities, and its practice where its followers already govern (Chapter 12).
>    - **What the condition does not forbid.** Every civil order binds everyone to its law. A free order's law is argued from the shared world. It is open to correction by evidence and by those who disagree, and it is drawn by citizenship rather than by confession. A creed's followers may argue for laws on those grounds, like any citizens. What the condition forbids is the demand that the creed's own customs, held beyond correction, become law for those outside it.
>    - **The supporting principle.** Principle 1. A creed that already holds the answer to how every man must live, and waits only for power to impose it, has closed the question that freedom of belief keeps open. It does not seek truth; it waits for power.
>
> **The civilization that espouses freedom of religion faces the same swap.** It keeps one boundary toward every creed in the same position:
> - it protects the apostate, the critic, and the preacher alike;
> - it keeps no blasphemy law for any creed;
> - it does not silence a man preaching in the open air because others threaten violence against him;
> - its civil law governs, in fact, what it claims in word: marriage as a civil status, custody, inheritance, and crime;
> - it applies whatever rule it keeps to every creed alike, a single spouse among them;
> - it grants no creed a reprieve from its civil law;
> - it makes any exemption by its own law, for everyone who meets the exemption's terms. Some societies have excused from bearing arms those who object to all violence. That excuse is the society's grant; no creed can claim it as a right.
>
> **On the preacher.** The High Court set the rule in *Redmond-Bate v Director of Public Prosecutions* (1999).
> - **The facts.** Three women were preaching from the steps of Wakefield Cathedral, by agreement with the police. A hostile crowd of more than a hundred gathered. A constable ordered the women to stop and arrested them for breach of the peace. One of them was convicted of obstructing him.
> - **The judgment.** The High Court allowed her appeal. Sedley LJ wrote: "Free speech includes not only the inoffensive but the irritating, the contentious, the eccentric, the heretical, the unwelcome and the provocative provided it does not tend to provoke violence. Freedom only to speak inoffensively is not worth having."
>
> **Where the civil law withdraws from its own domain, it can no longer claim to be the one law.** Something else will govern there. A freedom kept only for creeds that offend no one is not freedom of religion.
>
> **The test reaches every creed alike, religious or secular.** That reach is itself the swap. A philosophy that punishes those who leave it, silences its critics, or imposes its plan of life when it takes power fails as a religion does. The Soviet state is the plainest secular case. Creeds have branches, and the branches answer the swap differently. The test is applied to the texts and authorities that a given body of followers obeys.
>
> Three relations follow, in the tiers of Principle 4.
> 1. **One polity.** The followers of creeds that meet the four conditions can share one civil order under freedom of religion.
> 2. **Neighbours.** A creed whose followers do not meet them can found and keep its own civilization. With that civilization others can trade, and keep treaties where verification operates (Section 5 below).
> 3. **Enemies.** Whoever uses force against others is an enemy, of whatever creed. That is a judgment of acts under Principle 4, not a test of belief.

### A.2 The earlier conclusion draft, with the optional paragraph on religious liberty

> ### 3. Where We Get Along
>
> Reality is shared, but beliefs are not.
> - **The creeds contradict one another.** One creed teaches that a man is saved by grace through faith. Another teaches that he reaches the Garden by belief and righteous deeds, under the will of God. A third teaches that the goal is the extinguishing of craving, and with it of rebirth. These are not three wordings of one belief.
> - **The disagreement can be read in two ways.**
>   - The first way treats every creed as a dim view of one vague good and counts the differences as small. That reading honours none of the creeds. It sets each aside for a belief of its own, and carried to its end it holds that nothing is worth affirming.
>   - The second way takes each creed at its word, and grants that where two contradict each other, both cannot be correct. This book has taken the second way. It has quoted each creed from its own texts and authorities.
>
> Taking each creed at its word does not settle how its followers are to live beside one another. People who disagree about the next world must still share roads, markets, courts, and borders in this one. They need principles by which to decide how to act. Those principles cannot wait until every dispute about God is resolved. Chapter 9 derived the conditions under which people of different creeds can share freedom of religion. They are not the conditions of civilization itself. A creed whose followers cannot meet them can keep a civilization of its own. The conditions:
> 1. exit;
> 2. criticism;
> 3. covenants, not jurisdiction;
> 4. the same in power.
>
> All four are one test, the procedural symmetry of Principle 5. Would the creed accept, applied to itself, the rule it applies to others in the same position? That is the question at entry and exit, in speech, in promises, and in power.
>
> **[optional]** Religious liberty, as the English-speaking peoples came to practice it, was first argued among Christians. Locke opened his *Letter Concerning Toleration* on "the mutual toleration of Christians in their different professions of religion." He held that "neither Pagan nor Mahometan, nor Jew, ought to be excluded from the civil rights of the commonwealth because of his religion." He also bounded toleration by what a creed demands of the citizen. A liberty first framed among creeds of one moral inheritance may not hold unchanged across every creed and philosophy. Which creeds can share one civil order is a question to be answered, not a premise to be assumed. **[end optional]**
>
> The answers sort every relation into three tiers.
> 1. **Fellow citizens.** With the followers of every creed that meets the four conditions, a people can share one law, one court, and one army. Their citizens can hold any belief, leave it, and argue it in public.
> 2. **Neighbours.** With a civilization whose law is drawn by confession, a people can trade, keep treaties where they can be verified, and live at peace. It grants that law no jurisdiction inside its own borders. It does not pretend the two orders are one.
> 3. **Enemies.** Against whoever uses force, of any creed, a people defends itself.
>
> These tiers govern ordinary conduct as well as policy.
> - **A citizen** deals truthfully and keeps his word with men of every creed, because the covenant condition binds him as it binds them.
> - **A household** joins associations, chooses schools, and makes partnerships with people whose creed teaches them to keep covenants with outsiders.
> - **A town** grants no court, tax, or penalty to any creed's customs, and no reprieve from its own law. It protects the apostate, the critic, and the open-air preacher alike.
>
> This book does not set out to diminish any faith or philosophy, and it is not a theology. It is a rubric and a strategy for civilization: a way for any people to judge what will keep and build their common life, and with whom they can build it.

### A.3 Source checked: Locke

John Locke, *A Letter Concerning Toleration* (1689), in William Popple's translation, from the constitution.org text. It is saved in `ext-ref/locke/toleration/tolerati.htm`, with the University of Chicago *Founders' Constitution* excerpt beside it. Every Locke quotation above was checked against this text. The passages, in the order they appear in the *Letter*:

- **The opening line:** "Since you are pleased to inquire what are my thoughts about the mutual toleration of Christians in their different professions of religion".
- **The breadth:** "neither Pagan nor Mahometan, nor Jew, ought to be excluded from the civil rights of the commonwealth because of his religion."
- **Moral rules:** "no opinions contrary to human society, or to those moral rules which are necessary to the preservation of civil society, are to be tolerated by the magistrate."
- **Civil privilege, and reciprocity:** Locke denies toleration to those "who attribute unto the faithful, religious, and orthodox, that is, in plain terms, unto themselves, any peculiar privilege or power above other mortals, in civil concernments". He denies it also to those "that will not own and teach the duty of tolerating all men in matters of mere religion". Such men, he says, "only ask leave to be tolerated by the magistrate so long until they find themselves strong enough to effect it".
- **Allegiance:**
  - "That Church can have no right to be tolerated by the magistrate which is constituted upon such a bottom that all those who enter into it do thereby ipso facto deliver themselves up to the protection and service of another prince."
  - The reason: "the magistrate would give way to the settling of a foreign jurisdiction in his own country".
  - His example: "It is ridiculous for any one to profess himself to be a Mahometan only in his religion, but in everything else a faithful subject to a Christian magistrate, whilst at the same time he acknowledges himself bound to yield blind obedience to the Mufti of Constantinople".
- **Atheists:** "Lastly, those are not at all to be tolerated who deny the being of a God. Promises, covenants, and oaths, which are the bonds of human society, can have no hold upon an atheist."
