## 1. Reality

- Reality is what is. There is one Reality.
- In the strict sense, "Reality" means "our real, knowable, and consequential reality that we live in" (11-02).
- Reality includes "at least what is physically around us" (10-31). The letters do not say Reality is only the physical.
- A book or story does not create a new reality. It creates a narrative (11-04).
- Reality has its own nature, and it pushes back (11-04).
- Reality comes first. Models, statements, and beliefs are adjusted to match it, never the reverse (civ Principle 1.4).

## 2. Truth

- Truth is what corresponds to Reality. It is not what corresponds to a narrative (11-04).
- Truth has one frame. "Truth does not have different frames, just one: Reality" (11-04). Truth relative to a frame is not Truth.
- Reality is the truth-maker. A model of Reality is the truth-bearer. Models can be statements or actions. A model that corresponds to Reality is true; one that does not is false (11-02). Brad agreed with these definitions (11-03).
- Correspondence can be exact or approximate, depending on the task. Kepler's model corresponded further than what it replaced, and less than what replaced it (10-17). Newtonian mechanics stays true and useful within the range where its predictions match reality (civ Principle 1.4).
- Truth is self-coherent, but self-coherence does not make something true. "Truth is self-coherent (logical) but not all logic is truth" (10-19). Logical and true are two axes. Things placed on them do not collapse into a single line (11-01).
- In casual speech, the author knows what people mean by "fictional truths." In the strict sense the phrase is an oxymoron (11-01, 11-02).
- Alston's formula, which the paper takes for granted, does not name reality. The author's definition does, and he counts that as a real difference (11-01). Brad reads Alston's "what the statement is about" as referring to reality (11-01).

## 3. Logic

- Logic defines the relationship between things (11-04). It is "the set of relations we can draw within reality" (10-17). Logic is found within the world, not outside it (10-19).
- Logic is real and useful. Logic detached from reality is hallucination (10-17). Smart people have built self-coherent worlds, from explicit fantasy to the many realities of string theory, that are aimed at nothing (10-17).
- Logic can run with no external truth. A program can contain `if x == 3 then` without any claim about the world (10-19).
- Logic uses "true" in a narrower sense: true within a given model or story. That is different from Truth. "Logic can tell you what is congruent inside of a story but it cannot tell you if the story is Real" (11-04).
- A story may be self-consistent (11-04). Many stories are not (said while reviewing the 11-04 letter).
- The laws of logic are not in dispute (10-19). The three classical laws are reasonable as rules for keeping statements consistent with one another (10-17).
- The Law of Identity, as stated, is true but not interesting. `a == a` has the same term on both sides. Useful identity laws have another term, as in `a * 1 = a` or `a + 0 = a` (10-19). The first reaction called it "a non-operation posing as a useful law" (10-17).
- The laws are not merely linguistic tokens. There must be meaning behind the tokens (10-17). On this point the author agrees with the paper.

## 4. What kind of thing a law is

- The law of gravity is not a thing. Mass and the structure of spacetime are things. "The law of gravity" is our description of how they behave (11-04).
- The law of non-contradiction is the same kind of law. "It is not a thing Reality obeys; it describes Reality being what it is: a thing is never both what it is and what it is not, at the same time and in the same respect" (11-04).
- In truth-maker and truth-bearer terms, Reality is the truth-maker, and the law of non-contradiction is the truth-bearer describing it (11-04). On this framing, the statement of the law is true because it corresponds to Reality. What the author rejects is that the law is an entity in its own right, that it exists necessarily, or that it is a thought.
- "Like the hardness of rocks, it comes from the nature of Reality, not from a mind thinking it" (11-04).
- The 11-04 letter says "is never," not "cannot." As worded, it states what is the case in Reality and makes no claim about other possible worlds.
- The first reaction (10-17) compared the laws of logic with Kepler's and Newton's laws, to ask whether a statement can correspond to reality without being fully accurate. Brad called that comparison a category error (10-18). The 11-04 comparison with gravity makes a different point. It is about what kind of thing a law is, not about how accurate it is.

## 5. Fiction and narrative

- Superman is not True in any context, including the story (11-02). The story disconnects itself from Reality on purpose. That is what fiction is.
- "It's true in the fictional narrative that Superman is from Krypton" uses "true" in the narrower, logical sense: truth within the story (11-04).
- The story itself exists in Reality as words on a page or sounds in the air. They can be known. They can trigger neurons, and the neurons make a difference (11-04).
- What the narrative describes is not True. The story did not happen in Reality (11-04).
- A story can describe things that are Good. A story can contain truth, for example by correctly describing rocks as hard (11-04).
- The story's content is not consequential. The author of the story controls what happens to the characters. He could have described the rocks as hard or as bouncy, and the story's rocks are not intrinsically hard. A narrative does not push back, because its author controls it (11-04).

## 6. Consequential

- "Consequential" means that Reality pushes back on its own. It does not mean only "has effects." Brad heard "has effects," and that was the source of the misunderstanding (11-04).
- Running into a wall breaks a nose. Doing nothing still ends in starvation or death from exposure. Reality pushes back even if no one acts (11-04).
- "Physical" is close to what he means. He avoids "action," because it suggests a will or a deliberate movement (11-04).
- Reality is never at rest. Air particles and molecules are always moving. "Reality is consequential because there is no way to escape the activity of the physical" (11-04).
- Cause and effect operate regardless of intention. Dry cotton burns when a flame reaches it, whatever the person holding the flame intended (civ Principle 1.3).

## 7. Knowledge and justification

- The starting point is not Cartesian. "I don't start from 'I think'. I start from 'I interact and Reality pushes back.'" (11-04)
- The starting point is what anyone can see: we exist in the world and interact with it (11-01, 11-02). The author is not a radical skeptic and not a fatalist (11-02).
- We know Reality through proxies (11-04):
  - Eyes are matter arranged to turn bands of light into nerve signals. Ears do the same with variations in air pressure.
  - Humans build proxies: X-ray machines, telescopes, Neuralink.
  - Proxies can be chained. A radiograph turns X-rays, which the eye cannot see, into an image the eye can see.
  - We learn about our proxies by testing them in different ways.
- Models converge under testing. From Ptolemy to Einstein, models of the planets and stars converged on more accurate predictions. Newton joined the model of the planets with the model of motion on earth (11-04).
- Truth and justification are different. "Correspondence is what makes a belief true. Justification is having a reason to think it corresponds" (11-04).
- A belief is justified to the degree that it has been tested against Reality and holds up, through the senses and instruments and through predictions that continue to come true. The more independent tests converge, the stronger the justification (11-04).
- A method can be justified while the explanation given for it is not. Farmers find that crop rotation works and explain it by saying "the spirits demand it." The rotation is justified, because crops grow better. The explanation is not. Later testing justified the explanation by nitrogen-fixing bacteria in legume roots (11-04).
- A demonstration outranks an argument. Against a flat-earth claim, arguments and math are possible, but a Starlink receiver taking a connection from orbiting satellites is the better answer: "Your arguments can't make this happen. Mine can" (11-04).
- From civ Principle 1.2: intuition may suggest a hypothesis but does not confirm one. Religious revelation may add a claim, but it may not cancel a measurement, and the obligation to test it remains.

## 8. Necessary, contingent, and extrapolation

- Claims about what lies beyond the universe's beginning, and claims about "all possible worlds," cannot be tested by repeated testing against Reality. That is why they are extrapolation (11-04).
- "First mover" and "contingent world" arguments are extrapolation from the world we interact with (11-02).
- The author does not grant, as a premise, that the physical world is contingent (10-31). He does not assert the opposite either. "I'm not saying the world is or is not contingent" (11-02). "Nor am I saying the reality as we know it must not be contingent" (11-01). What he disputes is the framing.
- His objection concerns the world as a whole. He has not addressed whether things within the world, such as buildings or works of art, are contingent (Brad asked on 11-01).
- An argument meant to prove that God exists cannot assume that God exists. Making the physical world contingent forces a god-like being into the premises. If the paper's argument depends on the world being contingent, then that, not logic, is the argument actually being made (11-01).
- With no revelation, only observation, a person would know that he exists in the world (11-01). Brad disputes that this is a neutral starting point (11-01).
- The first reaction held that necessary and contingent are distinctions inside an essentialist picture, and that they do not survive defining truth as correspondence with reality (10-17). Brad asked for an argument that the distinction is not meaningful (11-02). The later letters state the objection as a refusal of the framing (11-02), not as a claim that the distinction is meaningless.

## 9. God, the physical world, and the paper's disjunction

- The letters do not claim that God does not exist. Whether God made the world is a further question (10-19).
- A real God and a real created world do not exclude each other (10-19). The author rejects the paper's disjunction: "If physicalism is true, there is no God; if God exists, physicalism is false" (paper, p. 12).
- He does not see why God must be defined as non-physical. He offered thought experiments, not beliefs: God might exist in more dimensions and enclose creation in three, God might be the essence of the spacetime fabric, or reality might be a simulation from God's perspective. In each case, what he rejects is that the exclusion is necessary (10-19).
- If the universe began without a god, it would be reasonable for it to exist in a consistent, logical manner. If God created a real world, its consistency would be expected in the fabric of the world itself, not as God's thought. Placing logic outside the world diminishes what God's creation actually is (10-19).
- The first reaction argued from Christian texts. Romans 1 says what may be known about God is plain in the things that are made, and the world of Genesis 1 is physically real. "If God is real and knowable, as Romans 1 says, then let God be real" (10-17). This criticizes the paper on Christian terms. It is not a statement of the author's own faith.
- Some Christians speak as if God were the author of a story, not the Creator of the universe and all that is real. That view is no different from Islamic occasionalism: like an author, God could decide the rocks are bouncy tomorrow (11-04).
- The author put a question to Brad: if rocks are hard by their own created nature, not because God narrates them hard moment by moment, could the laws of logic likewise be grounded in the nature of Reality rather than being thoughts? (11-04)

## 10. Where the author agrees and disagrees with the paper

Agrees:

- There are laws of logic, and the three classical laws are reasonable (10-17, 10-19).
- The laws are not merely linguistic tokens (10-17).
- Brad's eight-step summary represents the paper's argument well (10-31).

Disagrees:

- **The switch (p. 4).** The paper allows the laws to be relations. It then says there must be truths about the relations, and it asks the reader to substitute "the truths about the laws of logic" for "the laws of logic," calling the disagreement merely semantic. The author refuses that substitution. Logic and truth are different things (10-19, 10-31, 11-01). Fiction shows why the difference matters: a story can be logical and not True. If the two collapse, we cannot speak separately of what is logical but not aligned with Reality, and of Reality itself (11-01).
- **Essentialism.** The first reaction read the paper's claim that propositions bear truth-values by nature, "just as particles bear mass-values," as essentialism brought in without being named or argued for (10-17). The paper begins with an essential being and works backward to physical properties (10-17). The author starts at the other end: Reality exists, and momentum passing between two balls is the cause of their motion (10-19).
- **The contingency premise (section 6 of the paper).** Not granted. See section 8 above.
- **The physicalism disjunction (p. 12).** Rejected. See section 9 above. The paper does not define physicalism (10-19). Brad supplied the standard definition, that all things that exist are physical (10-31).
- **That the laws "really exist" in the same sense as the pyramids (section 4 of the paper).** The laws are descriptions, not entities. See section 4 above. The first reaction rejected the paper's second, third, and fourth arguments for their existence, and held that what makes a difference is Reality, not logic apart from it (10-17).
- **Intentionality (section 7 of the paper).** He questions the category. Are a language model's outputs, or a program full of logic, intentional entities? A category is useful as a short label for a group that is already real. It misleads when it is built to bring something to life (10-17).
- **Scripturalism.** The paper's conclusion is functionally close to W. Gary Crampton's Scripturalism, which Anderson rejects in print. The author cannot square how Anderson holds a realist view of truth and reaches so similar a conclusion (10-19).
- **Honesty.** The first reaction judged the paper not honest about the switch (10-17). The 10-19 letter called that reaction "long" and "raw." The later letters argue the substance and do not repeat the charge.

## 11. Christianity: reasons he does not accept it as true

From the letter of 11-04, answering Brad's question "What are your main reasons to not accept Christianity as true?"

The governing claim: "Christianity makes claims that should show up in the knowable world. When I check those claims they don't appear to show up."

1. **Transformation.** Christianity claims the Holy Spirit transforms believers. He does not observe Christians changing more than non-Christians do by will and by choosing different environments.
2. **Genesis.** Read literally, Genesis makes claims that can be tested in geology and biology, and when tested, those claims fail. He believes Jesus treated Genesis as literal.
   - Some Christians answer that God intervened. His example is the Institute for Creation Research's RATE project.
   - Some old-earth Christians say Genesis is figurative or a poem. That leads him to ask what else they take as figurative, and the answer is often a great deal.
   - "If every claim that fails a test is reclassified, then Christianity itself is suspect."
3. **Physical healing and falsifiability.** Humans are limited, and not knowing some things is acceptable.
   - He grants: "God is God and He doesn't have to do anything."
   - Christians speak of the Holy Spirit at work, and of God as powerful and willing to defend. Yet when someone is shot or deeply cut, he does not see God heal in a physical, predictable way. Christians usually point to inward healing.
   - His examples: Charlie Kirk was not physically healed after being shot. President Trump's survival as he turned his head is called a miracle, and "This could be God or it could be chance."
   - One death is called God's will, and another's survival God's miracle. "All things are God's will, so nothing can be falsified."

His conclusion: many aspects of Christianity have worked socially. Christianity helped build Western civilization as we know it, and forgiveness is useful. He asks whether the explanation for that is justified, as in the crop-rotation case, and answers: "At least in my current knowledge and understanding, my answer is no."

He knows there are answers to these objections and has heard many of them. In his opinion, those words do not align with Reality, and they do not build bridges, computers, or industrial processes. "People who test Reality build things that work." The frustration he states is with his situation generally, not with Brad or the conversation.

## 12. What the letters do not claim

- That God does not exist.
- That the physical world is not contingent.
- That everything real is physical. "Physical is close" describes what he means by consequential. Civ Principle 1 allows both a physical-only reality and one that includes God and spiritual laws, provided both accept a single shared physical world whose properties do not change when descriptions change.
- That logic is unreal, unimportant, or in dispute.
- That fiction has no effects. A story has effects through the real artifact, but its content does not push back.
- That necessary and contingent are meaningless distinctions. The later letters refuse them as a framing and a premise.
- That all Christians are young-earth creationists, or that Christianity has no social value.
- That his conclusion about Christianity is final. It is stated "in my current knowledge and understanding."

## 13. Questions the letters leave open

These are questions raised by the position, or likely to come from Brad, that the letters have not answered.

- Whether anything real is non-physical, and if so, what.
- Whether there are truths where no truth-bearer exists. For example: before any humans existed, was it true that 2+2=4? The paper's footnote 32, quoting Plantinga, holds that truth cannot be independent of noetic activity (activity of a mind).
- How the law of non-contradiction is known, whether by testing as gravity is known or in some other way, given that every test assumes it.
- How the standard of justification, testing against Reality, is itself justified.
- How graded correspondence, as with Kepler and Newton, fits with the law of excluded middle. The first reaction raised this (10-17), and it has not been resolved.
- How the nature of Reality (rocks are hard by their nature) differs from the essentialism the author rejects in the paper.
- Whether things within the world, such as buildings, are contingent (Brad asked on 11-01).
- What evidence, beyond personal observation, the transformation and healing observations rest on.
- The resurrection, which the letters have not addressed.
- Brad's eight-step list (10-31), which has not been answered step by step.

## 14. Relation to civ Principle 1

- The letters work out each part of Principle 1: *real* (section 1), *knowable* (section 7), *consequential* (section 6), and *truth is what corresponds to reality* (section 2).
- Principle 1.4 cannot accept coherence alone, meaning a theory that is consistent on paper but fails against the physical world. That is the distinction between logic and truth, and it is the ground of the objection to the switch.

- Principle 1.2 says revelation may add a claim but may not cancel a measurement, and the obligation to test it remains. The reasons in section 11 apply that test.
- Principle 1.2 says sensory contact with reality and reason are linked. The account of proxies and convergence in section 7 spells out how.
