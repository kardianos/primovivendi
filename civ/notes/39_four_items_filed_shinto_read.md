## 6. Six ways to view Shinto from the framework

Each names the passage of the book that supplies it.

1. **Remove the section.** The author's suggestion. "Shinto" appears in no other chapter and not in `civ/ccp_sheet.md`, so no cross-reference is left hanging. Alternate (a).
2. **By the root test.** The section above applies one test to Buddhism (`12_ch12.md:139`): an ideal that is the root of a civilization has to sustain itself when people attain it. Shinto would be asked the same question. That needs a source for what Shinto asks of a household, and none is saved.
3. **By the practice against the explanation.** Chapter 2 separates a sound practice from the account given for it (`02_ch02.md:99-105`). Chapter 3 leaves the ultimate questions open and tests what claims something about the world (`03_ch03.md:74-76`). The clean workshop and the kept tool are judged by results. The kami and the descent are an account, and they claim nothing testable until they are made history or law. This view keeps the credit of the first paragraph without calling the beliefs aligned with truth.
4. **By where the law's scope is drawn.** Principle 5, as the chapter applies it to Islam (`12_ch12.md:117-119`) on the finding of Chapter 9 (`09_ch09.md:55-63`). This is the framework's own verdict on Shinto. `civ/notes/02_book1_4.md:103` has "Shinto & Blood-and-Soil Nationalism: Binds legal standing and truth entirely to ethnic myth/descent, failing Principle 5". The documents of section 4 carry it, with no claim about other peoples or about atrocities. Alternates (b) and (c).
5. **By the ruler in a scope of his own.** Chapter 9 draws the consequence for truth: "A ruler who answers to no one can declare the sky green, and his subjects find it hard to pursue truth" (`09_ch09.md:93`). "Sacred and inviolable" is that case. Principle 1 is then reached by the book's own argument, and "silenced internal rational dissent" can go. To name an act of suppression would need a source that is not read.
6. **By the state's construction.** Kuroda's finding: the state made an independent Shinto from 1868 by separating the kami from Buddhism and calling the result non-religious, and the Occupation abolished it in 1945. On this view State Shinto has a beginning and an end, and "Shinto as a whole" is what came before it and continued after it. This answers the instruction to evaluate Shinto as a whole first. It has no need of "Meiji and Showa militarists".

One point holds under every view. The passage offers "a particularist folk religion" as the cause of the failure. Under Principle 2 people are particular, and under Principle 4 what each keeps is his own first. A tradition that belongs to one people is not a defect by the book's own principles. The failure has to come from the scope of the law or from a claim closed to proof.
