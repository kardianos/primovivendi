## 4. Value symmetry in chapter 9 (note 15, call 2)

**Author's direction:** the 2026-11-13 framing is "the best framing and articulation of the differences yet, including the two cautions." The sheet change is applied. Chapter 9 needs "a full corrected treatment."

### The framing, as accepted

Both are symmetries. They differ in what they hold equal.

- **Value symmetry** holds the *losses* of two sides equal. A wrong is a debt between families or tribes, paid by an equal loss on the other side.
- **Procedural symmetry** holds the *procedure* equal for every person in the same position. A wrong is a breach of the law, answered by a penalty fixed beforehand for the act.

Three tests follow:

1. **Who bears the penalty.** Under procedural symmetry, the person who did the act. Under value symmetry, anyone who balances the ledger: a brother, a son, or a daughter.
2. **Who judges.** Under procedural symmetry, a third party with no stake. Under value symmetry, the injured side, or a court acting on its claim.
3. **What sets the measure.** Under procedural symmetry, the act, fixed beforehand. Under value symmetry, the worth of the loss to the injured side, which is why it grades the penalty by rank, tribe, or religion.

Two cautions:

- Restitution measured by the actual loss, such as lost wages or a burned barn, set by a court under rules stated beforehand and paid by the person who caused it, is not value symmetry. The counterfeit prices the person, not the loss.
- A talion applied by a court to the offender himself, the same for everyone, is procedural symmetry with a severe penalty. The grading is the counterfeit, not the talion.

A third party is necessary and not sufficient. A written code applied by judges can still be value symmetry.
