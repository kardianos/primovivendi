## 3. Original Research: Real-Life Candidates for the Granary Slots

### G1 (`02_ch02.md:102–104`): Separating Working Practice from Folk Explanation

- **Best Candidate: Nixtamalization (Lime-Treated Maize) and the American Pellagra Epidemic (1900–1940)**
  - **The real-life case:** Mesoamerican peoples boiled and soaked dried maize in water with wood ash or slaked lime for millennia as a traditional food custom without knowing vitamin biochemistry. The alkaline bath chemically frees bound niacin (Vitamin B3) and tryptophan so the human body can absorb them. When European colonists and later Southern U.S. farmers adopted maize as a cheap staple crop while discarding the traditional lime-soaking custom, pellagra swept Europe and the American South, sickening an estimated 3 million people and killing at least 100,000 in the U.S. South in the early twentieth century. Dr. Joseph Goldberger (U.S. Public Health Service, 1914–1929) proved the cause was dietary rather than infectious, and Conrad Elvehjem isolated niacin in 1937.
  - **Why it fits G1:** It is a literal, lethal historical demonstration of destroying a working practice because nobody knew its biochemical mechanism, and then discovering the real mechanism through controlled testing. It replaces a wholly invented "field spirits and clover" fable and removes one granary from Chapter 2.
  - **Primary sources to acquire:** Joseph Goldberger et al., *Public Health Reports* (1914–1920, NLM/PMC public domain); Clay, Schmick, and Troesken, NBER working paper on pellagra in the U.S. South; Conrad Elvehjem et al., *J. Am. Chem. Soc.* (1937).

### G3 (`09_ch09.md:98–102`): The Winter Storehouse (Scoped Borders and Reserves)

- **Best Candidate: Törbel, Switzerland: The 1483 Alpine Commons Charter and the "Wintering Rule"**
  - **The real-life case:** Törbel is an actual mountain farming village in Valais, Switzerland, whose residents signed formal articles on **February 1, 1483** governing their communal alpine summer pastures, forests, and irrigation channels. The 1483 charter explicitly barred outsiders (*Fremde*) from acquiring rights to the village commons simply by buying land; membership required the community's formal grant. A companion statute (the **wintering rule**, codified by 1517) decreed that *no citizen could send more cows to the common summer alp than he could feed through the winter* on his own stored hay. Documented in anthropologist Robert McC. Netting's *Balancing on an Alp* (1981) and Nobel laureate Elinor Ostrom's *Governing the Commons* (1990), Törbel's bounded commons survived intact for over five centuries without overgrazing or dissolution.
  - **Why it fits G3:** The current parable already invents "forty farming families in a cold mountain valley" storing winter supplies. Törbel is the *real* mountain farming village, with a 500-year historical record and a Nobel-winning institutional analysis proving that clearly defined boundaries and contribution-linked access are the literal prerequisite for a shared reserve to endure.
  - **Caveat:** Törbel is a *positive* example (boundaries kept, commons survived); it shows why boundaries are required rather than dramatizing an instant collapse from unbolting the doors. If Chapter 9 wants a *failure* case alongside it, Plymouth Colony's 1620–1623 common storehouse (see B5 below) or Sweden's November 2015 emergency border reinstatement could pair with it.
  - **Primary/scholarly sources to acquire:** Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (Cambridge University Press, 1990), ch. 3 ("Törbel, Switzerland", pp. 61–65); Robert McC. Netting, *Balancing on an Alp* (1981).

### G4 (`10_ch10.md:120–132`): The Seed Granary (Generational Theft vs. Virtue 7)

Two extraordinary real-life cases form an exact positive/negative pair:

- **Candidate 1 (Negative: Consuming the Inheritance): Nauru and the Nauru Phosphate Royalties Trust (1968–2004)**
  - **The real-life case:** Upon independence in 1968, the tiny Pacific republic of Nauru (population about 8,000–10,000) possessed rich finite phosphate deposits that gave it one of the highest per-capita GDPs in the world. Recognizing that the rock would run out, Nauru created the **Nauru Phosphate Royalties Trust (NPRT)** as a sovereign inheritance for future generations. At its peak around 1990, the trust held roughly **A$1.3 billion** (some estimates up to A$1.7 billion). Instead of keeping the capital intact, the living generation and government borrowed heavily against the trust to cover chronic public deficits, funded luxury imports, speculative overseas hotels, and a flopped London West End musical (*Leonardo*), while strip-mining roughly 80 percent of the island to barren limestone. By the early 2000s, Asian Development Bank assessments recorded the trust plunging by more than 75 percent into receivership and effective insolvency; creditors seized flagship assets like Melbourne's 50-story Nauru House, and the Bank of Nauru collapsed.
  - **Why it fits G4:** It is the exact literal modern equivalent of eating the seed wheat and borrowing against next year's harvest for a February feast, and it eliminates a third granary parable while remaining completely non-partisan.
  - **Primary sources to acquire:** Asian Development Bank (ADB), *Program Completion Report: Fiscal and Financial Reform Program (Nauru)* and Pacific Economic Monitoring reports; Helen Hughes, "From Riches to Rags: What Are Nauru's Options and How Can Australia Help?" (CIS, 2004).

- **Candidate 2 (Positive: Keeping the Seed Grain Under Extreme Hardship): The Vavilov Institute Seed Bank During the Siege of Leningrad (1941–1944)**
  - **The real-life case:** During the 872-day Siege of Leningrad, staff at the All-Union Institute of Plant Industry (founded by **Nikolai Vavilov**, who is already named in Chapter 2 at `02:65`) guarded tons of edible wheat, rice, peas, oats, and potatoes in the institute's world seed collection. According to institutional records published by Vavilov Institute officials S. M. Alexanyan and V. I. Krivchenko (*Diversity*, 1991), **nine botanists and staff members died of starvation** at their posts rather than eat the seeds reserved for future harvests and breeding.
  - **Why it fits G4:** It is literally a real-life seed granary, creates a striking thematic callback to Vavilov in Chapter 2, and gives Virtue 7 a real historical exemplar to stand beside George Washington (`10:118`).
  - **Primary source to acquire:** S. M. Alexanyan and V. I. Krivchenko, "Vavilov Institute Scientists Heroically Preserve World Plant Genetic Resources Collections During World War II Siege of Leningrad," *Diversity* 7, no. 4 (1991): 10–13.

### G5 (`11_ch11.md:102–116`): The Hill Town Grain Storehouse and the Rats (False Compassion Hurts the Weakest First)

- **Best Quantitative Anchor: Bureau of Justice Statistics (BJS) National Crime Victimization Survey (NCVS)**
  - **The real-life evidence:** Across multi-year BJS NCVS studies (*Household Poverty and Nonfatal Violent Victimization*), Americans in households at or below the Federal Poverty Level suffer nonfatal violent crime at **more than double** the rate of high-income households (e.g., 39.8 vs. 16.9 per 1,000 persons in the landmark BJS poverty report), with higher shares of serious violent crime and firearm violence, while reporting crimes to police at slightly higher rates than wealthier households (roughly 50–51% vs. 43–45%).
  - **Why it fits G5:** Note 21 (`21_literary_review_argon.md`) actually praised the Hill Town Grain Storehouse as one of the book's most vivid fables. Rather than cutting it completely (especially if G1, G3, and G4 become real-life cases, leaving G5 as the *only* grain storehouse fable in the book.), grounding `11:116` with the BJS NCVS federal statistics proves empirically that the fable's asymmetry ("wealthy stone bins vs. widows' pine bins") is real in modern cities.
  - **Primary source to acquire:** U.S. Department of Justice, Bureau of Justice Statistics, *Household Poverty and Nonfatal Violent Victimization, 2008–2012* (NCJ 248384, Nov. 2014) and the latest annual *Criminal Victimization* NCVS bulletin (bjs.ojp.gov).

## 4. Original Research: Real-Life Candidates for the Bridge Slots

All five engineering cases below come with official government accident reports or engineering inquiries, and match the book's plain engineering register directly:

### B1 (`02_ch02.md:13, 76, 90`): Consensus/Paperwork vs. Physical Reality, Blueprint Error, and Hidden Rot

Three distinct real bridge collapses fit the three exact points made in Chapter 2:

1. **FIU Pedestrian Bridge Collapse (Miami, March 15, 2018)**: *Best match for `02:13` ("village council votes the bridge sound")*
   - **Official source:** NTSB Highway Accident Report **HAR-19/02** (2019) and OSHA investigation report (2019).
   - **What happened:** Days after the 174-foot concrete truss span was placed over SW 8th Street, large structural cracks grew at nodal connection 11/12. At a project meeting on the morning of March 15, the engineer of record (FIGG Bridge Engineers) assured the client and contractor that the cracks posed **"no safety concern."** The road underneath stayed open while crews re-tensioned rods on the damaged node. At 1:46 p.m. the span collapsed onto waiting traffic, killing **6 people** and injuring **10**.
   - **Fit:** Replaces a fictional "village council voting a rotted bridge sound" with a real meeting that declared a cracking bridge safe hours before it dropped onto the road.

2. **Quebec Bridge Collapse (St. Lawrence River, August 29, 1907)**: *Best match for `02:76` ("blueprint specifies forty tons, bridge collapses under twenty") and `02:13` period tone*
   - **Official source:** *Royal Commission Quebec Bridge Inquiry Report* (Canada, Sessional Paper No. 154, 1908).
   - **What happened:** Consulting engineer Theodore Cooper lengthened the main cantilever span to 1,800 feet and raised permissible stresses without recalculating the bridge's own dead weight, which ran thousands of tons over the original estimate. When site inspector Norman McLure measured visible buckling in lower compression chord **A9L** and traveled to New York to warn Cooper, construction continued until chord A9L buckled and dropped the span in 15 seconds, killing **75 of 86 workers** on the bridge. Cooper's stop-work telegram arrived moments too late.
   - **Fit:** Matches the 1860s–1950s treatise register seamlessly and shows reality vetoing an erroneous blueprint and a prestigious expert's authority.

3. **First Tay Rail Bridge Collapse (Scotland, December 28, 1879)**: *Alternative for `02:13` or `03:82–84` (concealed defects)*
   - **Official source:** Board of Trade Court of Inquiry (*Rothery Report*, 1880).
   - **What happened:** The central high-girder spans collapsed in a winter gale under a passenger train, killing **75 people** (no survivors). Commissioner Henry Rothery ruled the bridge "badly designed, badly constructed, and badly maintained." Testimony revealed foundry workers at Wormit had patched casting blowholes in defective iron columns with **"Beaumont egg"** (a putty of beeswax, rosin, lamp black, and iron filings) and painted over them to pass inspection.
   - **Fit:** A classic Victorian primary-source case of falsified physical inspection meeting a storm.

4. **Silver Bridge Collapse (Point Pleasant, West Virginia, December 15, 1967)**: *Best match for `02:90` ("calculation does not show hidden internal decay")*
   - **Official source:** NTSB Highway Accident Report **HAR-71/01** (1970).
   - **What happened:** During rush hour, a hidden **0.1-inch stress-corrosion crack** inside the pin hole of non-redundant **eyebar 330** fractured suddenly, dropping 31 vehicles into the Ohio River and killing **46 people**. The flaw was inaccessible to visual inspection and led directly to the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1968 and the National Bridge Inspection Standards.
   - **Fit:** Literally real-life proof of `02:90` ("the calculation does not show it") and `03:92` ("our observations can miss a hidden crack").

### B2 (`02_ch02.md:123–125`): Incomplete Model Pushed Outside Its Domain

- **Tacoma Narrows Bridge Collapse (Washington, November 7, 1940)**
  - **Official source:** Federal Works Agency Board of Engineers (Othmar H. Ammann, Theodore von Kármán, and Glenn B. Woodruff), *The Failure of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge* (March 28, 1941).
  - **What happened:** Designers applied static suspension-bridge load equations that worked well for heavier, stiffer spans with open trusses, but replaced the stiffening truss with shallow 8-foot solid plate girders. In a **42 mph** wind well below its rated static wind load, aeroelastic torsional flutter tore the span apart.
  - **Fit:** A sharp real-life engineering illustration of an *incomplete* model working inside its domain (static loads on stiff bridges) and failing when extrapolated beyond that domain without accounting for aerodynamic forces.

### B3 (`02_ch02.md:151`): Bounded Error and Safety Margins

- **Brooklyn Bridge Wire Fraud and Roebling's Factor of Safety (1878)**
  - **Historical sources:** New York and Brooklyn Bridge Trustees Investigation (1878); Washington Roebling's reports to the Board of Trustees (summarized in David McCullough, *The Great Bridge*, and AISC/engineering histories).
  - **What happened:** In 1878, engineers discovered that wire contractor **J. Lloyd Haigh** had diverted wagons of inspected steel wire and substituted hundreds of tons of rejected, brittle wire already spun into the four main cables. Because John A. Roebling had engineered the cables with a conservative **factor of safety of 6** (some accounts say 6 to 8), Washington Roebling calculated the degraded margin (still around 4), forced Haigh to pay for replacement stock, and added **150 extra sound wires** to each main cable rather than demolishing the span. The bridge opened in 1883 and still carries traffic today.
  - **Fit:** Concrete real-life upgrade for `02:151` ("sizes the span to carry several times the heaviest wagon expected, and the bridge stands"). It shows why bounded uncertainty is managed by margins, not paralyzing skepticism.

### B4 (`03_ch03.md:92`): Admitting Error Openly Rather Than Hiding a Structural Defect

- **Citicorp Center Structural Crisis: William LeMessurier (New York, 1978)**
  - **Historical sources:** Joe Morgenstern, "The Fifty-Nine-Story Crisis," *The New Yorker* (May 29, 1995), based on LeMessurier's contemporary engineering calculations and records; NSPE / ASCE engineering ethics case studies. *(Note: Verify primary technical details via ASCE/NIST/LeMessurier accounts before quoting verbatim.)*
  - **What happened:** Prompted by an engineering student's question a year after the 59-story skyscraper opened, structural engineer **William LeMessurier** recalculated diagonal ("quartering") wind loads and discovered that the contractor had substituted bolted joints for welded ones on the chevron braces without his approval, leaving the tower vulnerable to collapse in a **16-year storm** if power failed to the tuned mass damper. Rather than hiding the error to protect his reputation, LeMessurier reported it immediately to Citicorp and city officials; welders worked nightly to weld two-inch steel plates over more than 200 joints before Hurricane Ella approached.
  - **Fit:** Directly answers `03:92` (admitting a structural miscalculation openly to the people affected rather than protecting pride), and uses a skyscraper instead of repeating another bridge.

### B5 (`08_ch08.md:50–62`): The Family Creek-Bridge (Tiered Family Incentive vs. Confiscatory Egalitarianism)

Two real-life historical cases make the exact point of Chapter 8 without repeating a bridge *and* cleanly show the economic outcome of removing vs. restoring family-anchored rewards:

1. **Plymouth Colony Ends the Common Course (1623)**
   - **Primary source:** William Bradford, *Of Plymouth Plantation*, Book II, Anno 1623 (public domain; readily acquirable into `ref/`).
   - **What happened:** For its first two years, Plymouth farmed under a "common course and condition" into a common storehouse. Young men resented laboring for other men's wives and children without reward, women pleaded weakness, and harvests fell short. In spring 1623 Governor Bradford assigned every family a parcel of land "for their own particular." Bradford recorded: *"This had very good success, for it made all hands very industrious, so as much more corn was planted than otherwise would have been... The women now went willingly into the field, and took their little ones with them to set corn."* Famine ended permanently.
   - **Fit:** A primary-source American historical case that proves Principle 4's core claim (people invest labor and care because it is for *their own household*) and directly answers the egalitarian theorist on Bradford's own terms (Bradford explicitly refuted Plato's communalism in the same passage).

2. **Xiaogang Village, Anhui Province, China (December 1978)** *(secondary option, already well-known; requires checking Chinese/scholarly primary record if used)*
   - Eighteen farming households in a starving commune signed a secret thumbprint pact to divide collective fields into family plots; grain output in 1979 matched the previous five years combined, catalyzing China's Household Responsibility System.

### B6 (`09_ch09.md:154–160`): Abolishing Anonymous Examinations for Identity/Class Outcomes

- **China's Abolition (1966) and Restoration (1977) of the *Gaokao* National University Entrance Exam**
  - **Historical record:** At the start of the Cultural Revolution in **1966**, the Chinese government abolished the national merit entrance examination (*gaokao*) as elitist and bourgeois. When universities reopened in **1970–1976**, admissions ran on political recommendation and class background ("worker-peasant-soldier" students) rather than standardized testing. The result was a lost decade of technical, scientific, and engineering training. After Mao's death, **Deng Xiaoping** restored the anonymous merit examination in late **1977**.
  - **Fit:** Replaces or anchors the hypothetical "university dean discarding the Structural Mechanics exam" (`09:154–160`) with a nation-scale historical experiment, and eliminates another bridge reference in Chapter 9.
  - **Primary sources to acquire:** Deng Xiaoping's August 1977 remarks at the Forum on Science and Education (*Selected Works of Deng Xiaoping*, vol. II) and State Council restoration notice (October 1977).

## 6. Varied Subject Suggestions: One Signature Object Per Lesson

Where a short, self-contained illustration works better than a full historical case study, the prop-repetition problem can still be solved cleanly by giving each slot **one distinct physical subject** that does not collide with any existing parable in the book (checked against all current parables: map, cow stall, lifted shingle, cold forge, chalked footrace, two hangars, shepherd's paddock, tradesmen's insurance group, locked shop door, concert hall choir, and walker in the marsh).

That lets the author and reader refer back to any illustration unambiguously by its single subject name ("the bridge example," "the granary example," "the cistern example," "the orchard example," "the levee example").

### Recommended Ownership of the Two Existing Names

* **"The bridge example" belongs to Chapter 2 (`02:5–151`).** Bridges are already built into Chapter 2's opening structure (13 mentions). Keeping the bridge as Chapter 2's signature subject (with or without historical anchors such as Quebec, FIU, Silver, or Brooklyn) frees Chapters 3, 8, 9, and 11 to use distinct subjects.
