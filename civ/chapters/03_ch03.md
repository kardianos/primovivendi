## Chapter 3: The Rivals to Correspondence — Scripturalism, Occasionalism, and Constructivism

### Why Correspondence Must Be Defended at the Root

To a practical builder—a carpenter, a machinist, a surgeon, or a farmer—the principle that *reality is real, knowable, and consequential, and truth is what corresponds to reality* sounds almost too obvious to need defending. Who in his right mind would deny that a claim is true when the world is the way the claim says it is?

Yet across history and in our own time, several influential intellectual and theological systems have explicitly rejected correspondence to an observable, regular world. They differ sharply in their vocabulary—one speaks the language of Protestant theology, another the language of medieval Islamic metaphysics, and a third the language of modern university literary and political theory—but all three perform the exact same structural amputation: **each removes the external, observable world as the shared test that can correct human belief.**

Let us examine each of the three fairly and see why no civilization can build on them.

### 1. Scripturalism Versus the Two-Books Tradition

Within Christian thought, the decisive epistemological question is whether the physical universe God created is a reliable, knowable witness to truth.

For the overwhelming majority of Christian history, the answer has been an emphatic **yes**—a position classically known as the **Two-Books Tradition**. In this view, God is the author of two books: the Book of Scripture (special revelation) and the Book of Nature (the physical universe). Because both books have the same divine Author, the observable physical world is real, orderly, and intelligible to human senses and reason. As the Apostle Paul wrote in his Epistle to the Romans (1:20): *"For his invisible attributes, namely, his eternal power and divine nature, have been clearly perceived, ever since the creation of the world, in the things that have been made."* Four centuries later, **Augustine of Hippo**, in *On Christian Doctrine*, urged Christians to claim and use whatever was empirically and logically true in pagan natural philosophy and science—comparing it to the Israelites taking gold and silver vessels out of Egypt to put them to proper use—while warning Christians never to bring the faith into disrepute by talking nonsense about astronomy or natural history that any careful observer of the sky or the earth could falsify by direct observation. It was this conviction—that the created universe operates by stable, rational laws laid down by a Lawgiver—that animated natural philosophers from Robert Grosseteste and Roger Bacon to Johannes Kepler, Robert Boyle, and Isaac Newton.

In the twentieth century, however, a narrow philosophical school known as **Scripturalism**—developed by the American philosopher **Gordon H. Clark** (1902–1985) and popularized by writers such as **W. Gary Crampton**—rejected the Two-Books settlement and rejected empirical correspondence altogether.

In the strict Scripturalist epistemology, human sensory observation can never yield genuine knowledge of anything; empiricism is dismissed as a total fallacy. According to Crampton and Clark, truth is not correspondence to an observable physical reality; rather, a proposition is true solely because God thinks it, and human beings possess truth *only* through the 66 books of the Bible and formal logical deductions drawn directly from biblical sentences.

Notice how pious that formulation can sound in a seminar room, and notice what it actually does the moment you step outside the room to build a bridge, set a broken bone, or try a defendant for murder:

1. **It severs belief from empirical correction.** The Bible does not contain the stress tables of structural steel, the wiring diagram of a cardiac pacemaker, or the factual answer to whether John Smith robbed the bakery on Elm Street last Tuesday at 10:00 p.m. To find out whether John Smith robbed the bakery, a court must rely on eyewitnesses, security cameras, fingerprints, and physical evidence—every single one of which is sensory observation! If sensory observation cannot yield truth, then no courtroom trial can ever establish guilt or innocence, and no engineer can ever verify whether a bridge is safe.
2. **It mistakes coherence for correspondence.** By reducing all human knowledge to axiomatic deductions from a text while denying the Book of Nature any standing as an independent witness, Scripturalism leaves rival interpreters of the same text with no external reality check when their deductions collide with observable facts.

### 2. Ash'arite Occasionalism Versus Secondary Causes

A second major anti-correspondence package arose within Islamic theology during the tenth and eleventh centuries: **Ash'arite Occasionalism**, formulated by **Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari** and defended with formidable philosophical brilliance by **Al-Ghazali** (c. 1058–1111) in his famous treatise *The Incoherence of the Philosophers* (*Tahafut al-Falasifah*).

Wishing to defend the absolute, unconstrained omnipotence of God's will against Greek-influenced philosophers who spoke of immutable laws of nature, Al-Ghazali argued that created physical things possess **no causal power or stable nature of their own whatsoever**. There is no necessary connection between what we call a "cause" and what we call an "effect."

In Al-Ghazali's famous example, when you bring fire into contact with a piece of dry cotton, **the fire does not cause the cotton to burn**. Fire is an inanimate thing incapable of action. Rather, at the instant the fire touches the cotton, God directly wills and creates the blackness in the cotton and the separation of its fibers into ash. God could just as easily touch fire to cotton and refuse to create the burning. What human beings call "laws of nature" are not objective properties built into physical reality; they are merely temporary customs or habits of divine will (*'adat Allah*), which can be altered or suspended at any moment because will, not nature, is the sole cause of every event.

Here again, we must make an honest distinction:

* First, the Islamic intellectual tradition was never a single monolith. Before and during Al-Ghazali's time, strands of Islamic thought such as **Mu'tazilite rationalism** and the *falsafa* tradition defended secondary causes and rational regularity. A generation after Al-Ghazali, the great Andalusian jurist and philosopher **Ibn Rushd (Averroes, 1126–1198)** wrote *The Incoherence of the Incoherence* (*Tahafut al-Tahafut*), warning explicitly that anyone who denies objective secondary causes and natures in physical things denies the possibility of human knowledge altogether—because to know a thing *is* to know its causes and stable properties.
* Second, where an occasionalist theologian adds the qualification that God's habit is steady enough in ordinary life for a farmer or a physician to rely on it, everyday craft can continue and the dispute remains metaphysical.

Where Ash'arite occasionalism becomes fatal to civilization is when it is preached as a public epistemology that places unbound divine or clerical will above observable cause and effect. If fire has no intrinsic power to burn cotton and steel has no intrinsic tensile strength, then investigating the material properties of the world is no longer a path to truth; every outcome—a plague, a lost war, a collapsed economy, a failed harvest—is reassigned directly to inscrutable will rather than traced to physical and institutional causes that human beings must take ownership of and repair.

### 3. Postmodern Constructivism: Truth as a Move of Power

The third major rival to correspondence requires no theology at all; it dominates huge swaths of modern Western humanities departments, schools of education, and administrative bureaucracies. This is **Postmodern Social Constructivism**, associated with theorists such as **Michel Foucault** (1926–1984) and **Jacques Derrida** (1930–2004) and carried into institutional practice by critical theory.

The constructivist begins from a partial truth (which we will examine closely in Chapter 5): human beings use language to categorize the world, and institutions with political power often shape which categories are favored. From that sensible observation, however, strong constructivism leaps to a totalizing claim: **there is no objective, independent reality accessible behind language, and "truth" and "knowledge" are nothing more than artifacts of social power ("power-knowledge" or *pouvoir-savoir*).**

In this view, scientific method, biological sex, objective merit, and the rule of law are not discoveries about how reality works; they are hegemonic narratives constructed by dominant groups to preserve their privilege. Therefore, when a statement is advanced—whether about biology, crime rates, historical records, or engineering standards—the constructivist does not ask, *"Does this statement correspond to external facts?"* He asks, *"Whose political group-interest does this statement advance, and whose power does it subvert?"*

Watch what happens to human speech and social cooperation the moment truth is subordinated to power (whether by secular constructivism or by a theological-political system of unbound will):

1. **Language ceases to be a shared map and becomes a weapon.** Under Principle 1, words exist to describe reality so limited people can coordinate and check one another's errors. Under constructivism, words are instruments used to move an audience, secure resources for an in-group, or silence a rival.
2. **Disconfirmation becomes impossible.** If a constructivist policy is enacted—say, abolishing advanced math classes or ceasing to prosecute theft—and test scores plummet while shops board up their windows, those disastrous physical results can never falsify the theory. Instead, any citizen who points to the boarded-up shops or the falling math scores is accused of bad faith, "false consciousness," or bigotry. Every contradiction in reality is recoded as a moral sin in the observer.
3. **Limited scope dissolves into total conflict.** In a society that accepts objective reality, two neighbors can disagree peacefully about private beliefs while agreeing on whether the street is paved and the contract was paid, because the physical world serves as a neutral referee outside both of them. Once you declare that "everything is political" and that "truth is constructed by power," there is no neutral referee left in the universe. The only way to establish "truth" is to seize the microphone, capture the bureaucracy, and force everyone else to repeat your narrative.

### The Public Square Rule: The Church and State of Knowledge

How does a free, productive civilization allow believers of different faiths and non-believers to live and build together without falling into either Scripturalist blindness, Occasionalist fatalism, or Constructivist power-warfare?

By enforcing a clean jurisdictional boundary—what we may call the **Church and State of Knowledge**:

* **For any claim that can be tested in observable reality, bring it to trial in reality, and let reality's verdict stand.** Claims about whether a bridge design will carry a load, whether a medicine cures an infection, whether a defendant committed a burglary, or whether an educational method teaches children to read are empirical claims in the shared physical world. They yield to counting, measuring, and controlled testing. No sacred text, no clerical decree, and no ideological narrative may override a failed inspection report on a physical bridge. Nobody's God is insulted by an honest measurement of reality.
* **For ultimate metaphysical questions that leave no empirical trail in the shared test plot, civilization's common principles issue no catechism and no anti-catechism.** Whether the universe was created by God, what happens to the soul after death, or how an individual prays in his household are questions beyond the scope of a wind tunnel or a municipal accounting audit. People who differ on those ultimate questions can still build an electrical grid, serve on a jury, and defend a border together—*provided* they all hold that the shared world is real, knowable, and consequential.

Picture two civil engineers from different religious traditions arguing over how to design a river bridge. If the first engineer says, *"We must make the piers out of unreinforced mud brick because my ancient scroll says so,"* and the second says, *"I reject your scroll; we must build it according to my scroll,"* they are not arguing about engineering at all, and they can never reach agreement until one conquers the other. The moment both engineers agree that **the river current, the weight of the trucks, and the compressive strength of the masonry get the final veto**, they have stepped onto the common ground of Principle 1. Now they can test the mud brick and the stone in water, watch the mud brick dissolve, and build the bridge in stone together.

### Deriving Virtue 1 (Align with Truth) and Virtue 5 (Be Humble)

Look at what Principle 1 immediately demands of you and me as individual actors.

Blueprints do not draw themselves, accounting books do not balance themselves, and laboratory dials do not write down their own readings. A living human being has to look at the world and report what is there.

Suppose an aircraft structural engineer runs a fatigue test on a wing spar and sees that the metal cracked at 80 percent of the required load. If he reports the crack honestly, the project will be delayed by six months, his supervisor will be angry, and his annual bonus will be postponed. If he falsifies the logbook and writes down that the wing passed, everyone in the office goes home happy on Friday afternoon.

Why is falsifying that logbook a mortal sin against civilization? Because reality is consequential. The falsified paper satisfies the human bureaucracy, right up until the physical airplane flies into a thunderstorm three years later, the wing snaps off in midair, and two hundred passengers fall to their deaths.

Therefore, Principle 1 directly forces our first personal virtue of civilization:

> **Virtue 1: Align with truth.**

To align with truth is the daily personal discipline of conforming your mind, your measurements, your accounting, and your speech to reality as it actually is, rather than to what is flattering, comfortable, or politically convenient. It means checking the cash in the drawer against the ledger before you spend it. It means stepping on the bathroom scale and reading the number without making excuses. It means never cooking the books, never rigging a test, and never repeating a fashionable lie to buy applause from a crowd.

At the same time, look closely at the person who is trying to align with truth. Even when we are completely honest, we do not know everything. Our instruments have tolerances, our observations can miss a hidden crack, and our favorite mental models can be incomplete. When the check shows that our prediction was wrong—when the bridge settles, the business loses money, or the experiment fails—we face a choice: we can protect our pride by hiding the failure and rewriting what we claimed, or we can bow to reality, admit the error out loud to the people it affected, and correct our model.

That readiness to let reality correct our fallible minds—and to learn from the tested methods of those who built before us rather than assuming we know everything already—is the beginning of our fifth virtue of civilization, which we will develop even more deeply in the next chapter:

> **Virtue 5: Be humble.**

Hold Principle 1, Virtue 1, and Virtue 5 together. The world is real, knowable, and consequential, and truth is what corresponds to it. We align our minds and words with that truth, and we stay humble enough to test our ideas and accept correction when reality says we are wrong. Now we are ready to turn our eyes from the world around us to the creature standing inside it: **who is the human builder?**
