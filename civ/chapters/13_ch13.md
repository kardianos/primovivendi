## Chapter 13: Historical Cases from Singapore, Russia, and Iran

When we evaluate modern history under the Five Principles, three long-horizon criteria govern the inquiry.

The first is **the Generational Criterion (80 years)**. Never evaluate a policy by the intent behind it or by its immediate sentiment during a period of borrowed expenditure or revolutionary enthusiasm. A result comes from the policy, from the country it was enacted in, and from the people who carried it out. The three are not judged apart. Evaluate the policy by the condition of the country when the grandchildren inherit the resulting institutions and balance sheet eighty years later.

The second is **the Scale Criterion**. A voluntary commune, monastery, or early kibbutz of eighty selected members can sometimes operate for a few decades on informal communal sharing (Tier 2/3 *kindness*), because every member dines in the same hall and idlers can be corrected or expelled in person. If one attempts to scale that communal arrangement linearly to a nation of fifty million strangers without scoped procedural symmetry and private property (Principle 4), it collapses into bureaucratic coercion and mass deprivation.

The third is **the Interconnect Criterion**. The Five Principles are not an arbitrary collection from which one may retain two principles and discard three. Truth, realist human nature, keeping and building, tiered family and defense, and symmetrical courts depend structurally upon one another like the stones of a masonry arch.

Three twentieth-century historical cases demonstrate how these principles operate in practice: **Singapore (1965–1990)**, **Russia (1907–1933)**, and **Iran (1978–1983)**.

### 1. Singapore (1965–1990): Statecraft Under Resource Scarcity

If a fatalist or an ideological determinist had sought one territory in the world in **August 1965** that appeared destined for poverty, ethnic warfare, and foreign conquest, he would have selected the island of **Singapore**.

The physical and political facts on **August 9, 1965**, the morning Malaysia expelled Singapore from the Federation and Prime Minister **Lee Kuan Yew** addressed the nation, were severe. Two million people crowded onto a tropical island of 224 square miles of mudflats and marshland, with no oil, no coal, no iron ore, no agricultural hinterland, and an insufficient domestic supply of fresh drinking water; its water arrived through pipelines from Johor in Malaysia, which could sever the supply at will. The population was deeply divided by ethnicity, language, and religion (approximately 75 percent Chinese speaking several mutually unintelligible dialects, 14 percent Malay Muslim, and 8 percent Indian Tamil and others), and violent **Chinese-Malay race riots** had killed dozens in the streets in 1964.

At the same time, militant Communist trade unions paralyzed the docks with strikes, backed by revolutionary movements across Southeast Asia, while Indonesia's Sukarno conducted undeclared military sabotage (*Konfrontasi*) against the island. Within three years, Britain announced that it was withdrawing its naval base and military garrison, which had accounted for twenty percent of Singapore's economy and all of its external defense.

Every prevailing academic theory of the 1960s, including "Dependency Theory," socialist state ownership, post-colonial grievance politics, and ethnic majoritarianism, predicted that Singapore would become an impoverished, disorderly dependency.

Instead, within a single generation, Singapore rose from poverty to achieve one of the highest per-capita incomes, lowest crime rates, highest educational standards, longest life expectancies, and busiest ports and financial centers in the world.

**How was this achieved?** Lee Kuan Yew and his founding cabinet (Goh Keng Swee, S. Rajaratnam, Hon Sui Sen) rejected prevailing ideological illusions and structured their policies around the Five Common Principles of Reality:

#### 1. Consequential Reality and Ownership (Principle 1, Virtue 2)

Days after independence in 1965, Lee Kuan Yew stated the foundational premise of Singapore's existence to its citizens:

> *"The world does not owe us a living. We cannot live by the begging bowl."*

Lee refused to indulge in political excuses: he devoted **no time** to lamenting British colonial rule, demanding foreign reparations, or blaming external structural forces. He addressed physical reality directly: two million people on a resource-poor island had to produce goods and services so reliable, sound, and economical that the rest of the world would choose to trade, bank, and manufacture in Singapore rather than elsewhere.

#### 2. Particularity, Limits, and Excellence (Principle 2, Virtue 3)

While Western universities in the late 1960s increasingly embraced egalitarian leveling, Lee Kuan Yew spoke directly about human particularity and the necessity of cultivating high character and technical excellence. In 1966–1967, addressing school principals and students, Lee compared civic character to metallurgy:

One cannot construct a durable edifice out of soft pig-iron; a society must temper its citizens, both in physical stamina and moral character, into *"steel."*

Lee addressed the reality of human cognitive and executive variation (Principle 2). He noted that in any population, the most capable, disciplined, and public-spirited five to ten percent of citizens act as *"the yeast, the ferment, the catalyst"* that enables the entire society to advance. If a nation persecutes, drives out, or levels down its most capable engineers, administrators, and entrepreneurs in the name of envy, the remaining ninety percent do not become wealthier. They sink into unemployment and poverty. Therefore, Singapore established rigorously meritocratic schools, technical institutes, and civil service examinations. In them, one scholarship went to the son of a manual laborer who scored at the top of the mathematics examination. Another went to the son of a banker.

#### 3. Self-Anchored Ownership Over Unconditional Welfare (Principles 3 and 4)

Singapore provided for housing, healthcare, and retirement without adopting unconditional welfare transfer systems that subsidize family breakdown and idleness. It did so through the **Central Provident Fund (CPF)** and the Housing and Development Board (HDB) Home-Ownership Scheme. Instead of taxing working citizens to fund an anonymous pool from which free riders could draw benefits without working, Singapore required every working citizen and his employer to contribute into that citizen's own self-anchored, individual account for housing, medical care, and retirement.

Furthermore, Lee insisted that families **own** their apartments rather than renting them as state tenants, because he wanted every citizen to have a stake in the country (Principle 2 and Principle 4). He tied it directly to National Service: a soldier whose family owned its home would be defending that home, not the property of the wealthy (Lee Kuan Yew, *From Third World to First*, 2000).

#### 4. Scoped Procedural Symmetry Across Ethnic Groups (Principle 5)

Lee Kuan Yew enforced **Scoped Procedural Symmetry** in a region where neighboring states took the opposite path. Nearby governments enacted ethnic quotas that restricted minority Chinese and Indians from universities and civil service posts, and rulers dismissed judges when a court ruled against the state.

Although ethnic Chinese comprised three-quarters of Singapore's electorate, Lee refused to make Mandarin the sole dominant state language over the Malay and Indian minorities. Instead, he made **English** the neutral, symmetrical working language of the courts, universities, and commerce, placing every citizen under the same procedural standard while connecting Singapore directly to international science and trade, while each child also studied his mother tongue (Mandarin, Malay, or Tamil) in school.

Within the courtroom and marketplace, English common-law contract and property rules were enforced with strict impartiality, and the Corrupt Practices Investigation Bureau (CPIB) pursued even ministers: a Minister of State, Wee Toon Boon, was convicted of corruption in 1975, and the Minister for National Development, Teh Cheang Wan, died in 1986 while under investigation, making Singapore one of the least corrupt jurisdictions in the world.

#### 5. Enemies Are Real (Principle 4)

When Britain announced its military withdrawal, Lee did not rely upon diplomatic appeals to the United Nations for security. Singapore requested military advisors from Israel, the only country willing to train its nascent armed forces in 1965, and built a universal male conscription force (**National Service**) on what Lee termed the **"Poisonous Shrimp" doctrine** (and later the "Dolphin" doctrine). A small island cannot conquer a large neighbor, **but it can make itself so well-armed, disciplined, and formidable that any aggressor will suffer prohibitive losses, remaining, in Lee's phrase, *"not a digestible morsel."***

#### The Analytical Caveat on Singapore: Adopt the Principles, Guard Against Demographic Decline

Singapore's record carries two cautions. First, adopt the foundational principles, not every emergency regulation. A large continental republic does not need, and should not copy, every municipal regulation or restriction that a small island city-state adopted during a 1960s communist insurgency. What any nation *can* and *should* adopt are Singapore's alignment with reality, meritocratic standards, self-anchored ownership accounts, public order, swift punishment of violent crime and corruption, and symmetrical commercial courts.

Second, modern Singapore shows its own vulnerability in having children. Because it became a high-cost, high-density urban economy in which young adults delay marriage and childbearing for corporate careers, its resident Total Fertility Rate has fallen below **1.0 child per woman** (0.97 in 2023 and again in 2024; Singapore Department of Statistics). Even Lee Kuan Yew in his final years warned that this demographic decline was the single greatest threat to Singapore's survival, demonstrating once again that **The Interconnect Criterion** admits no exceptions: a civilization must maintain all Five Principles and all Seven Virtues, or the eighty-year replacement horizon overtakes even the most capable material builders.

### 2. Russia (1907–1933): From Stolypin's Farms and Durnovo's Warning to Lenin's Ruin

**Russia between 1907 and 1933** is the case of a vast empire that destroyed itself by repudiating the Five Principles.

It is frequently assumed that Imperial Russia before 1917 was a static, backward society destined inevitably for Bolshevik communism. The actual historical record presents a far more instructive account regarding keeping and building, self-anchored family property, and the consequences when a state disregards strategic reality.

#### 1. Pyotr Stolypin's Property Reforms (1906–1911): Relying Upon "The Sturdy and the Strong"

When Russian peasants were emancipated from serfdom in 1861, most were not granted individual family title to their land. Instead, the village commune (the *mir* or *obshchina*) held the land collectively and periodically **redistributed strips of land** among households by family size. It forbade any peasant farmer from consolidating his strips, fencing his field, or adopting modern crop rotation without the approval of the village assembly.

The incentives of the communal *mir* under Principle 2 were self-defeating. Why would an industrious peasant spend three years draining a marsh, hauling manure, and planting fruit trees on his fourth strip if the village commune would confiscate that strip at the next redistribution and assign it to a negligent neighbor? The result was chronic agricultural stagnation, strip-farming inefficiency, and rural grievance.

In 1906, Tsar Nicholas II appointed **Pyotr Stolypin**, a capable and resolute provincial governor, as Prime Minister. Stolypin diagnosed the fundamental problem immediately: Russia's peasants were radicalized and impoverished because they had never been permitted to become self-anchored property owners (Principle 4 and Virtue 2).

Between 1906 and 1911, Stolypin enacted his landmark agrarian reforms, granting every peasant household the legal right to exit the leveling commune and claim **permanent, hereditary private ownership** of a consolidated family farmstead (*khutor* or *otrub*), supported by a Peasant Land Bank and voluntary resettlement to fertile lands in Siberia.

On **December 5, 1908**, Stolypin defended his land law before the State Duma against socialist levelers who demanded that the state confiscate all land. The government, he explained, had wagered not upon *"the wretched and the drunken,"* but upon *"the sturdy and the strong,"* because the strong personal owner was the barrier to revolution. In an interview with the Saratov newspaper *Volga* in October 1909, he stated what the reform needed in order to take root: *"Give the state twenty years of internal and external peace, and you will not recognize present-day Russia."*

In practice, over the next seven years (1907–1914), **the policy succeeded.** Millions of peasant households applied for private title; grain harvests, railway freight, cooperative banks, and industrial output expanded rapidly. Revolutionaries made repeated attempts on Stolypin's life, and in September 1911 Dmitry Bogrov, an anarchist who was also a police informant, shot him at the Kiev Opera House. Lenin had already seen what the reform threatened: he wrote in 1908 that if Stolypin's agrarian policy were sustained long enough, Marxists might have to give up any agrarian program at all (Lenin, *Collected Works*, vol. 15).

#### 2. Pyotr Durnovo's Memorandum (February 1914)

Stolypin had asked for *"twenty years of internal and external peace"* for the new property order to take firm root. Russia received only seven.

In **February 1914**, four months before the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand triggered the First World War, the former Interior Minister **Pyotr Nikolayevich Durnovo** submitted to Tsar Nicholas II a confidential thirty-page state memorandum that remains one of the most accurate strategic forecasts in modern history.

Applying strict Principle 1 realism, Durnovo warned the Tsar:

Russia had no strategic interest to gain from a general war with Germany that could outweigh the severe strain on Russia's incomplete railways, munitions factories, and rural economy.

In the event of a prolonged, exhausting war accompanied by military reverses at the front, Russia's liberal opposition, the educated parliamentary intelligentsia, would use wartime hardships to overthrow the monarchy, assuming that they could govern Russia as a peaceful parliamentary democracy.

**Durnovo then stated precisely what would follow.** Russia's educated liberal politicians possessed no executive experience and no support among the peasantry. Meanwhile, socialist agitators preached simple slogans of total land and factory seizure to a population in which Stolypin's property reforms were still only halfway complete. Within months, ruthless extremists would overthrow the liberal government, and *"Russia will be flung into hopeless anarchy, the issue of which cannot be even foreseen."*

The Tsar left Durnovo's memorandum unheeded, entered the war in August 1914, and every paragraph of Durnovo's forecast was verified by events between February and October 1917.

#### 3. Lenin's April Theses (1917), War Communism, and the War Against Reality

When **Vladimir Lenin** arrived at Petrograd's Finland Station in April 1917 and issued his **April Theses**, followed by the Bolshevik seizure of power in October, the new regime systematically inverted all Five Principles of Reality. Against Principle 1, objective truth and scientific impartiality were abolished in favor of *partiynost* ("Party-mindedness"). Against Principle 5, independent courts and symmetrical statutes were replaced by the Cheka's **"Revolutionary Conscience,"** which explicitly instructed tribunals not to look for evidence of what offense a defendant had committed, but first to ask what class he belonged to, what his parents were, and what his education was.

Against Principles 3 and 4, under **War Communism (1918–1921)** and later Stalin's **Forced Collectivization (1929–1933)**, the Bolsheviks waged war against the very class of competent, industrious family farmers (*kulaks*) whom Stolypin had enabled. The application of the term *kulak* was systematically destructive. In practice, authorities branded as a "class enemy" any peasant who kept his barn roof repaired, owned two cows or a metal plow, and harvested more grain per acre than his negligent neighbor. They stripped him of his home and tools, and shot him or deported him in unheated railcars to the Arctic wilderness.

The consequence in reality was famine. Once the competent farmers were executed or deported and the remaining peasants were forced back into state collectives where they owned nothing they produced, agricultural production collapsed and millions starved to death across the Volga, Ukraine (the *Holodomor*), and Kazakhstan, in one of the most fertile grain-producing regions in the world. Demographers estimate that for 1932–1934 the famine caused about 3.9 million deaths above the expected number in Soviet Ukraine and about 3.3 million in the Russian republic (Levchuk, Wolowyna, Rudnytskyi, Kovbasiuk, and Kulyk, *Nationalities Papers*, 2020). In Kazakhstan, where the famine began earlier among the nomadic herders, about 1.5 million died, more than a quarter of the republic's population (Sarah Cameron, *The Hungry Steppe*, 2018). Physical reality is indifferent to revolutionary slogans; destroy the producers and abolish ownership, and the granaries empty.

### 3. Iran (1978–1983) and the Mechanics of Modern Red-Green Tactical Alliances

**Iran between 1978 and 1983** illuminates one of the most instructive phenomena in modern politics: How can two movements whose positive doctrines directly contradict each other cooperate closely to overthrow an existing civilization?

#### 1. The Shah's Warning: "The Unholy Alliance of Red and Black"

In the 1970s, Iran under **Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi** was rapidly industrializing, building modern universities, expanding secular courts, and educating women. While the regime possessed real defects and faced domestic opposition, two incompatible forces combined in 1978 to overthrow the Iranian state. The **"Red" faction** comprised Marxist-Leninists and secular left-wing intellectuals: the Communist **Tudeh Party**, the Marxist-Leninist *Fedaiyan-e Khalq*, the Islamic-Marxist *Mujahedin-e Khalq* (MEK), and Western-educated progressive university students who sought a socialist workers' republic, gender egalitarianism, and the overthrow of Western capitalism. The **"Black/Green" faction** consisted of **Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini** and his militant clerical network, whose explicit goal was to abolish secular law, impose strict *sharia* gender segregation and veiling, execute apostates and those accused of "corruption in the land" (*mofsed-e-filarz*), and establish absolute rule by Islamic jurists (*Velayat-e Faqih*).

In doctrine, a Marxist feminist student in Tehran and a theocratic cleric disagreed on almost every positive question of how human beings should live. Why, then, did they march together in the streets of Tehran throughout 1978? They disagreed about what to build, and they agreed about what to pull down. Each wanted the Shah's government gone, and neither could bring it down alone.

The Shah identified their coalition accurately as *"the unholy alliance of Red and Black."*

What followed in Iran after the monarchy fell in February 1979 shows how such an alliance ends.

The Tudeh Communist leaders and secular progressive professors expected to share cabinet ministries in a new revolutionary government. Instead, between **1979 and 1983**, Khomeini's clerics used their control of the mosques, the street militias (*Komitehs*), and the new Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps to crush every rival. They closed every independent newspaper, purged the universities in the "Islamic Cultural Revolution," forced women back under the mandatory veil on pain of flogging, outlawed the Tudeh Party and leftist organizations, and arrested, tortured, and executed thousands of their former Marxist and student allies inside Evin Prison.

When two anti-procedural factions combine to overthrow an order of law, they do not coexist peacefully once the common obstacle is removed; the stronger and more ruthless faction immediately destroys the weaker faction.

#### 2. Red-Green Coordination in the West

There is a saying that an enemy of your enemy is your friend. There is no better demonstration of this than the functional arrangement between radical progressives and Islamists. Red is the radical left, which comes from Marx. Green is the Islamists. The two are not one movement, and no agreement records their cooperation. Here is how their cooperation works.

The first thing to show is that the two sides will organize one event together. The Muslim Association of Britain held its first protest with the Stop the War Coalition in London on 28 September 2002. The two had not agreed on priorities. The Association's leaflets put Palestine first and the war second, and the Coalition's leaflets put the two in the opposite order (Timothy Peace, 2015). On 15 February 2003 came the largest demonstration in British history. The same two organizations arranged it with the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament. The demonstration opposed the coming invasion of Iraq. Key figures of the Coalition sat on the central committee of the Socialist Workers Party, a party of *"the revolutionary Marxist tradition."* The Association's ideology is *"loosely based on the teachings of the Muslim Brotherhood"* (Timothy Peace, 2015). Red was the Socialist Workers Party, which comes from Marx. Green was the Muslim Association of Britain, which comes from the Brotherhood. Both sides had one enemy that day: their own government, which was preparing to invade Iraq. The larger enemy of both is the civilization that would oppose its own destruction. Neither red nor green can advance while it lasts. They disagreed about what to build, and they agreed about what to pull down.

The second thing to show is that the arrangement survives the contradictions between the two doctrines. On 10 November 2019 about 13,500 people marched through Paris in what its organizers called the "March against Islamophobia" (franceinfo, 10 November 2019). Islam is a religion, and you can disagree with a religion. The Collectif contre l'islamophobie en France (CCIF) had called the march with the New Anticapitalist Party and with libertarian communists (Mediapart, 3 November 2019). Signatories included the general secretary of the CGT trade union, the leader of Europe Écologie Les Verts, and the spokesman of the Communist Party. The Socialist Party stayed away (RTL, 7 November 2019; franceinfo, 10 November 2019). A year later the government dissolved the CCIF. In September 2021 the Conseil d'État upheld the dissolution. The court had found that the CCIF kept close ties with proponents of a radical Islamism that urges people to disregard certain laws of the Republic, and that its conduct was of a nature to provoke discrimination, hatred, or violence. The court found no acts aimed at provoking terrorism (decision no. 449215, paras. 9, 11, and 12). Red was the Communist Party and the anticapitalist party. Green was the CCIF. Both sides had one enemy there: the law of the Republic, which is written for every citizen alike and exempts no religion. That law is part of the civilization that would oppose its own destruction. Again they disagreed about what to build, and they agreed about what to pull down.

The last thing to show is where the arrangement ends. In Iran, Marxists and Islamists marched together against the Shah in 1978. Each wanted the Shah's government gone, and neither could bring it down alone. They disagreed about what to build. The Marxists wanted a secular socialist republic and equality between the sexes. The Islamists wanted religious law, the veil, and the execution of apostates. By 1983 Khomeini's clerics had outlawed the Tudeh Party and executed thousands of their former allies (section 1 above). Red and green part on religious law, on the place of women, and on who commands once the common enemy has fallen. There the stronger side destroyed the weaker.

Every case ends where the others end. Red comes from Marx, and green from Islam. They disagree about what to build, and they agree about what to pull down. Neither can advance while the civilization they live in lasts. That civilization, and not each other, is their common enemy, because it would oppose its own destruction.

> Radical progressive activists march alongside Islamist groups whose classical legal doctrine prescribes severe punishment for homosexuality and legal subordination for women and religious minorities because both share the immediate tactical goal of subverting and paralyzing the host country's order of law.

They will destroy the civilization that opposes them, such as the one this book describes.
