## Chapter 10: Children, Inherited Debt, and Replacement

Up to this chapter, suppose a society orders almost everything properly.

Suppose the citizens living today align with truth, take ownership of their workshops and streets, seek excellence in their trades, build beautiful towns, and remain humble before reality. Their bridges are well maintained, their courts are scrupulously fair, their granaries are full, and their crime rate is negligible.

There is only one omission: the adults of this prosperous city decide that raising children is too costly, too disruptive, and too inconvenient for their personal leisure and career schedules, so they have an average of 0.8 children per woman.

Now project the demographic arithmetic forward **eighty years**.

What does that prosperous city look like in Year 80?

For the first twenty years, the city actually appears *wealthier* in financial statistics, because adults in their thirties and forties who are not expending money or hours on infants, larger dwellings, school tuition, and family vehicles have large disposable incomes to spend on restaurants, travel, luxury apartments, and pets, while both spouses work sixty hours a week. Economists analyzing only ten-year gross domestic product figures applaud the expansion.

By **Year 35**, the primary schools and universities begin closing their doors. A couple with 0.8 children has 0.32 grandchildren: every 100 citizens in the grandparents' generation has been replaced by 40 in the children's generation and 16 in the grandchildren's generation, an 84 percent population decline in two generations.

By **Year 55**, the median age of the city exceeds sixty. Across the municipal water plant, the electrical grid substation, and the hospital operating room, no thirty-year-old apprentices exist to replace retiring hands. The master electrician climbing the ladder to repair a high-voltage transformer in a winter storm is seventy-two years old. The pension funds, government bonds, and retirement savings accounts that the childless sixty-five-year-olds carefully accumulated prove to be unredeemable paper or electronic ledger entries, because currency cannot bake bread, tend an invalid, or roof a house. Money is only a claim upon the current labor of young, working hands; if nobody raised and trained those young working hands thirty years ago, there is nobody in the bakery or the hospital to honor the claim at any price.

Finally, by **Year 80**, a neighboring civilization or migrant population that *did* raise three or four children per family, and that brings its own laws, language, and religion, simply crosses the unguarded border or purchases the vacant properties at auction. The city's constitution, libraries, scientific journals, and courts become historical artifacts studied by another people's grandchildren.

That is **the eighty-year replacement horizon**.

And it demonstrates why no list of personal virtues can be complete until it bridges the interval from one mortal lifespan to the next. From Principles 1, 2, 3, and 4, we derive the sixth and seventh Virtues of Civilization:

> **6. Have children**\
> **7. Form and listen**

Both virtues require detailed analysis.

### 1. Virtue 6: Have Children

**Have children** is an explicit, foundational virtue of civilization rather than a private preference like stamp-collecting or golf because of three invariable facts of reality. First, every human being is mortal (Principles 1 and 2). Within roughly eighty years, almost every human being currently reading this book, administering the courts, piloting the aircraft, and cultivating the land will be dead. Second, civilization cannot be stored on inanimate storage media or stone monuments (Principle 3). Suppose a library of engineering textbooks sits in an empty city with no living human minds to read, practice, and execute them. It is merely stacked paper awaiting decay, because civilization exists only within living human beings. Third, replacement requires slightly more than two children per woman (approximately 2.1 under modern low-mortality conditions) across the population as a whole. Keeping and building life requires healthy, well-formed families raising two, three, four, or more children so that the population continues with vigor.

#### Aiming at Full Life, Not Grim Demographic Duty

The manner in which a society conceives of and speaks about having children is consequential.

If one frames having children merely as a joyless civic burden (*"Endure eighteen years of hardship so the national pension bond does not default in 2075"*), few will be moved to undertake it, and the framing itself misrepresents human nature.

Normal men and women across ten thousand years have desired children, labored for them, and regarded their children and grandchildren as the fulfillment of their lives because humans are biological, relational creatures constituted to transmit life, and a household full of children (demanding, exhausting, and costly as it may be on any given evening) is one of the deepest sources of satisfaction, meaning, and enduring affection available to a mortal human being.

In the **Plymouth Colony** of Chapter 8, once each household received its own parcel of land, fathers and mothers took their children into the fields. They did not experience that labor as a penal sentence. Building a livelihood for their own children gave their strength and skill a clear, daily purpose.

At eighty, one grandfather and grandmother sit at the head of a holiday table surrounded by four adult children and twelve grandchildren and great-grandchildren; another eighty-year-old sits alone in a silent apartment with a television set and a stack of travel photographs from forty years ago.

Therefore, **Virtue 6 aims at *full life***: forming stable marriages between men and women, welcoming children into the world as a benefit rather than an inconvenience, and maintaining a culture and household economy in which young couples can marry in their twenties and afford a home and a family without first spending fifteen years paying off inflated credential requirements.

#### Testing "Not Ready" and "Cannot Afford" Against the Record

Three reasons are commonly given for putting off a child or refusing one: "I am not ready," "I cannot afford a child," and the child would be poorly off. Each is a claim about reality. Under Principle 1, reality is knowable, and these claims can be checked against what households have actually carried and how children have actually fared. The record does not make hardship unreal. An extra bedroom would be welcome. More food would be welcome. The means to pay a babysitter now and then would be welcome. Those wants are not the same thing as an inability to keep a child.

Is a child too costly to keep on modest means? The households that raised the generation born after the Second World War kept children on much less. In 1950 the median American family had an income of 3,319 dollars, about 36,000 dollars at 2025 prices; in 2025 the median was about 110,500 dollars (U.S. Census Bureau, Current Population Survey, Table F-5). In 1950, 35.5 percent of American housing units lacked complete plumbing, meaning hot and cold piped water, a bathtub or shower, and a flush toilet for the household's own use. In 1940 the share had been 45.3 percent, and by 1990 it was 1.1 percent (U.S. Census Bureau, Historical Census of Housing Tables: Plumbing Facilities). Yet the total fertility rate rose from 3.09 births per woman in 1950 to 3.77 in 1957, on less than half of the present median income and in a country where more than a third of homes lacked complete plumbing at the start of the decade (National Center for Health Statistics, *Vital Statistics of the United States, 1999*, Table 1-7). Those children were kept. Many of them are the parents and grandparents of the people now reading this book.

The same holds at the scale of one household. A diet built on oatmeal, butter, and eggs costs little, and it can carry a household through years when money is short. Lean years are a hardship. They are not an inability to keep a child.

Is a person who does not yet feel ready unable to begin? In 1950 the median American man married for the first time at 22.8 years of age and the median woman at 20.3; in 2024 the medians were 30.2 and 28.6 (U.S. Census Bureau, Table MS-2). The couples of 1950 began their households younger, with less money and less experience than the couples of today. Readiness, in the sense the claim uses it, was not a condition they waited for. Stated in general, "I am not ready" is an immature claim. It treats readiness as a state that arrives before the work, when readiness is formed by the work, as the smith's hand in Chapter 7 is trained by years at the anvil and not before them.

Would the child be poorly off? Around 1900, about 100 of every 1,000 American infants died before their first birthday (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, *Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report*, 1999, vol. 48, pp. 849–857). In 1950 the rate was 29.2 per 1,000 (National Center for Health Statistics, *Health, United States, 2016*, Table 11). In 2024 it was 5.52 per 1,000, and the highest rate among the maternal race and age groups that the report tabulates was 18.53, for mothers younger than fifteen (National Center for Health Statistics, *National Vital Statistics Reports*, vol. 75, no. 3, 2026). A child born now is about one-fifth as likely to die in infancy as a child born in 1950. Survival and plumbing are not all that a child needs, but they can be counted. On survival, every maternal race and age group in the 2024 report stands below the national rate of 1950. On plumbing, the share of homes lacking complete plumbing fell from more than a third to about one in a hundred. The claim that a child of today would be poorly off measures the child against the parents' present comfort, or against what their peers display, and not against what children have actually had.

What is usually being refused, then, is not the keeping of a child but a restructuring of life. The person who says he cannot afford a child often means that he does not want the change: he still wants to experience certain things, and a child would cost him those experiences. That is a statement about values. It is the valuing of experience that Chapter 6 distinguished from valuing life. To a person who values experience, a child is a luxury item, to be added after comfort is secured. To a person whose aim is to keep and build, the child is part of the life being built, and the family is built from what the household has, not from a surplus that arrives later. Building past the comfort one already has, into something greater, matters more than the same work done from inside that comfort.

The record does not settle every case. Some people are unable to bear children, and some are unable to marry; the section on Virtue 7 below gives them an honorable place in forming the young. Some households face hardship beyond anything the figures above describe. Each of those is a fact about a particular person or household. None of them supports the general claim that a child cannot be kept or will be poorly off, and the record of the last century stands against that claim.

#### Direct Authority and Progressive Autonomy: The Twenty-Year Progression of Raising a Child

Parents form a helpless newborn into a capable adult across three stages. In the first seven years, an infant or young child has no capacity to judge highway traffic, nutrition, or moral truth on his own. The parents therefore keep him within a predictable, authoritative household perimeter, feeding, protecting, teaching speech, establishing basic obedience, and showing that his home is safe, truthful, and affectionate.

Between roughly seven and fourteen, as the child's reason and physical coordination develop, the parents stop treating him as an infant and guide him into apprenticeship. They assign regular household duties (sweeping the porch, feeding the dog, assisting with meals, managing a small allowance), instruct him in academic and practical skills, allow him to compete and experience genuine failure, and teach him to take ownership of his mistakes rather than offering excuses.

Between fourteen and twenty, in adolescence and young adulthood, the parents progressively reduce direct supervision while holding the young man or woman accountable to adult standards of work, honesty, and self-control, preparing the young adult to leave the parental home and establish an independent household.

Two opposite parental failures disrupt this twenty-year progression. The first is permissive neglect, the failure to exercise authority: parents who refuse to set boundaries for a three-year-old, treating a young child as an adult peer who should "negotiate his own bedtime and diet," produce an anxious, undisciplined child who has never learned self-command.

The second is overprotective supervision, the failure to grant autonomy. Some parents continue to treat their twenty-two-year-old son or daughter as a fragile four-year-old. They contact an employer to complain when the young adult is corrected at work, shield him from every consequence, and forbid him to take physical or enterprising risks. By doing so, they prevent the capacity for independent adulthood from developing. One supports a young tree with a stake while its roots take hold in the soil; one then removes the stake so the tree can stand in the wind by its own strength.

### 2. Virtue 7: Form and Listen

**Having children** must be joined by forming and listening because biological birth alone does not transmit civilization, and because the young do not come to hold a method unless elders form them and they listen.

Elders form those younger than themselves. The duty is broad. It includes kin. It also includes politicians and other leaders, who train the people younger than themselves.

Suppose a million infants are born this year, and their parents feed them until they reach eighteen, yet withhold everything else. No one teaches them to read, calculate, or work metal; no one exemplifies marriage or honesty; no one keeps up the courts or the water mains. Meanwhile, the older generation runs up fifty trillion dollars of public debt to fund its own retirement and leaves the bill to the young. Those children were born biologically, but they were deprived of their civilizational inheritance.

Every human infant enters the world naked, illiterate, and ignorant of the five thousand years of accumulated human technology that sustain his life. Civilization therefore requires this virtue. Every living adult receives the inheritance, maintains it, and hands it on in working order, together with the moral and technical knowledge required to operate it.

#### Edmund Burke's Partnership Across Time

In 1790, France's revolutionary National Assembly abolished the old provinces, courts, and titles, confiscated the lands of the Church, and placed the clergy under a civil constitution. Its members acted on the error that living theorists alone held the right to dismantle their country and build society anew. In response, the Anglo-Irish statesman **Edmund Burke** wrote a passage in *Reflections on the Revolution in France* that states Virtue 7 clearly.

Burke observed that a real society is *not* a temporary commercial partnership in pepper, coffee, calico, or tobacco, to be entered into for a brief seasonal profit and dissolved at the pleasure of the current shareholders. Rather, society is a partnership in all science, all art, and every virtue, and because the ends of such a partnership cannot be obtained in many generations, it becomes:

> *"a partnership not only between those who are living, but between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born."*

Burke's observation applies directly to every individual life. Can you repay the dead who built your world? You cannot write a check to Michael Faraday for discovering electromagnetic induction so that your electric lamp operates tonight. You cannot repay the Roman jurists and English common-law judges who developed the rules of contract and habeas corpus, or the soldiers who died in the trenches more than a century ago so that you could mature speaking your own language in a free country. The people who gave you your language, your tools, your clean water, and your laws are in their graves.

In reality, a debt to the ancestors can only be paid forward to the descendants. You repay your great-grandfather for planting the apple orchard and defending the county courthouse by tending the orchard, defending the courthouse, and transmitting both (in sounder condition than you received them) to your children and grandchildren.

And what do the younger owe the elders? They seek the experience of those around them. They listen. They test what they are told against reality. They do not have to agree with all of it. A failed test is a reason to refuse a claim. A refusal to listen is not a test.

#### How Every Adult, Including the Unmarried and Childless, Fulfills Virtue 7

**Even if a specific individual is unmarried, infertile, or unable to have biological children of his own, he is never exempt from, or excluded from, Virtue 7.**

While biological parenthood (Virtue 6) is the primary means of biological continuation, **Form and listen binds every adult in the polity** and gives unmarried, widowed, or childless men and women a vital, honorable place in forming the younger and in handing the civilization on:

* A childless aunt or uncle who helps raise, mentor, and provide an inheritance to nieces and nephews;
* A couple unable to conceive who adopts or fosters children and gives them a disciplined, affectionate home;
* A dedicated schoolteacher, master machinist, coach, scientist, or pastor who spends forty years imparting accurate knowledge, craft excellence, and moral character to hundreds of young apprentices and students;
* A soldier, firefighter, engineer, or magistrate who defends the borders and upholds the laws so that every family's children have a stable country in which to mature.

A historical example is George Washington, who had no biological children of his own, yet raised Martha's children and grandchildren, spent his life establishing and defending the United States for hundreds of millions of future citizens, and twice gave up power he could have kept: his command of the army in 1783 and the presidency in 1797. That is the elder's side of forming and listening, practiced on a national scale. He formed those younger than himself, and he handed the inheritance on instead of keeping it for himself.

#### Refusing Generational Theft: Nauru and the Leningrad Seed Bank

The grave violations of Virtue 7 are three: elders who refuse to form those younger than themselves; the young who refuse to listen to the experience around them and test it; and **generational theft**, in which the living treat the accumulated moral, institutional, and financial capital of their ancestors as a private fund to be exhausted in a single lifetime, leaving decayed infrastructure, debased currency, massive sovereign debt, and corrupted schools.

Two twentieth-century records show the violation and the virtue in stark contrast:

> **Nauru's Phosphate Trust (1968 to 2004):** Upon independence in 1968, the small Pacific island of Nauru held rich, finite phosphate deposits that gave its roughly ten thousand citizens one of the highest incomes per person in the world. Knowing the rock would run out, the republic established the Nauru Phosphate Royalties Trust to preserve that wealth for future generations, and by about 1990 the fund stood above one billion Australian dollars. Instead of keeping the principal intact, the living generation borrowed against the trust to cover ongoing public deficits, bought luxury imports and speculative overseas hotels, and strip-mined four-fifths of the island to barren limestone. By the early 2000s, Asian Development Bank reviews recorded that the trust had lost more than three-quarters of its value and entered receivership, leaving the next generation with stripped land and an insolvent state.
>
> **The Leningrad Seed Bank (1941 to 1944):** During the 872-day Siege of Leningrad, staff at the All-Union Institute of Plant Industry, founded by the geneticist Nikolai Vavilov (Chapter 2), guarded tons of edible wheat, rice, peas, and potatoes stored as the nation's breeding collection. According to the institute's published records, nine botanists and staff members starved to death at their posts rather than eat the seed collection reserved for future harvests.

Any society that accumulates peacetime deficits so current voters can consume more than they produce, allows its power grids and roads to age without replacement, inflates away savings, or converts its schools into centers of ideological conditioning is repeating Nauru's error. Virtue 7 commands the opposite: preserve the seed, maintain the inheritance, and leave the next generation stronger than you began.
