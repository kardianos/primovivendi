## Chapter 12: Philosophies, Religions, and Political Systems

We now possess a complete, self-consistent analytical framework: the Five Common Principles of Reality and the **Seven Virtues of Civilization**.

It provides one test of compatibility for judging history, philosophy, religion, and politics. Two compatible doctrines can still be at odds, but a doctrine that fails is self-defeating or an enemy within.

Without an explicit set of foundational principles, people frequently evaluate philosophies and political movements by **factional label** or **rhetorical tone**: *"Does this writer employ persuasive language? Does he oppose the same adversaries I oppose? Does his treatise invoke familiar terms such as justice or tradition?"* Through such superficial criteria, large numbers of well-intentioned citizens are induced to adopt defective ideological systems, ranging from utopian Marxism on the political left to Eurasianist collectivism on the political right.

**Scoped procedural symmetry** and truth as correspondence to reality require the **same inspection checklist** for every philosopher, every religious framework, and every political movement, whether friendly or hostile, ancient or modern, Eastern or Western. We do not judge a doctrine by its outward title; we test its foundational premises directly and ask:

1. Does this system affirm that reality is real, knowable, and consequential, and that truth is correspondence to reality (Principle 1)?
2. Does it take human beings as they actually are: self-interested, limited, and particular (Principle 2)?
3. Does its moral standard require human life continuing, kept, and built (Principle 3)?
4. Does it structure duty as self-anchored, tiered, and outward while recognizing that enemies are real (Principle 4)?
5. Does it govern multi-household life by scoped procedural symmetry rather than arbitrary decree, tribal privilege, or forced leveling of outcomes (Principle 5)?

Major philosophical schools, world religions, and political doctrines can be evaluated directly under these five criteria.

### 1. Specific Philosophers and Philosophical Schools Graded by the Five Principles

#### A. Scottish Common Sense Realism (Thomas Reid, James Beattie, James Oswald)

As we saw in Chapter 2, the eighteenth-century Scottish Common Sense Realists, led by **Thomas Reid** (*An Inquiry into the Human Mind on the Principles of Common Sense*, 1764), alongside **James Beattie** and **James Oswald**, provide one of the clearest philosophical articulations of Principle 2 in Western thought, and one of its clearest answers to the skeptic who denies Principle 1.

Reid answered speculative philosophers such as René Descartes, John Locke (in his theory of "ideas" as mental representations), Bishop Berkeley, and **David Hume**. They claimed that humans never perceive the physical world directly and cannot know causation or external reality, and Reid saw clearly that they were undermining the foundation on which all human inquiry depends. Reid showed that every skeptic relies in practice on his senses, memory, and reason the moment he leaves his study to cross the street or take a meal. He also grounded active moral agency (Will) and practical duty in the real constitution of human nature.

Scottish Common Sense Realism became the foundational curriculum of early American colleges, such as Princeton under John Witherspoon, who taught James Madison.

#### B. Classical Constitutionalism (John Locke, James Madison, James Wilson)

The architects of classical Anglo-American constitutionalism combined Scottish Realism and English common-law procedure to solve the central problem of Principle 2 and Principle 5: how to establish a strong government administered by flawed, self-interested mortals without permitting the rulers to plunder the citizens.

**John Locke** (*Second Treatise of Government*, 1689) rightly recognized that life, liberty, and property (the fruit of a person's labor mixed with the earth) exist prior to the state, and that people form governments to act as an impartial procedural magistrate rather than remaining in a state of private blood feuds. Locke's theory of knowledge was weaker: his *tabula rasa*, or "blank slate," and his indirect theory of perception. Thomas Reid supplied the corrective, and **James Wilson**, a Scot trained in Common Sense Realism and one of the primary drafters of the U.S. Constitution, carried it into his *Lectures on Law*.

**James Madison** (*Federalist* Nos. 10 and 51) is the foremost political engineer of Principle 2: instead of assuming that elections would fill Congress with selfless angels, Madison designed separated branches, bicameral checks, federalism, and enumerated scopes of jurisdiction so that *"ambition must be made to counteract ambition."*

#### C. Burkean Conservatism (Edmund Burke, Russell Kirk)

As we saw in Chapter 10, **Edmund Burke** and twentieth-century successors such as **Russell Kirk** articulated several of these core truths: humility before the limits of human knowledge, the difficulty of building an institution compared with the ease of destroying one, and attachment to country beginning in what Burke called the *"little platoons"* of family and neighborhood (Principle 4). They also stated the discipline of forming and listening: elders form those younger than themselves, and the younger listen and test, inside the partnership of the dead, the living, and the unborn.

Burkean respect for inherited custom must remain anchored to truth as correspondence with reality, and to building as well as keeping. If "tradition" is detached from empirical verification, a society risks preserving defective customs rather than repairing and advancing its methods.

#### D. Classical Stoicism (Hierocles, Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius)

The Roman Stoics excelled at self-command and at taking ownership. **Epictetus**, born a lame slave in Hierapolis, taught the distinction between what is within our control (our own judgments, integrity, and actions) and what is not, and he forbade a man to evade responsibility by blaming others. The Emperor **Marcus Aurelius**, author of the *Meditations*, kept to his civic and military duty on the Danube frontier when he would have preferred the quiet study of philosophy at home. Stoic detachment fails where it becomes resignation and treats the material world as indifferent, because then it gives a man no reason to build. Self-command serves civilization when Principle 3 supplies its purpose.

**Hierocles**, a Stoic of the second century, drew each person at the center of concentric circles, in a fragment that Stobaeus preserved (*Anthology* 4.27.23). The nearest circle holds his own mind and body. Then come parents, siblings, wife, and children; then the wider kin; then fellow citizens and countrymen; and last the whole human race. Hierocles called the relations of these circles unequal, and he granted that greater distance in blood takes away some goodwill. His instruction was to draw the circles together toward the center, honoring the people of each circle as though they belonged to the circle inside it. This book takes his map and not his instruction. Under Principle 4 the nearer circles rightly command the larger share.

#### E. Confucianism (Confucius / Kongzi, Mencius)

Across more than two millennia in East Asia, Confucianism proved extraordinarily durable at reinforcing Tier 2 and Tier 3 family stability, filial listening across generations, disciplined study (Virtue 3), and polite social order.

Its weakness shows in the Duke of She's sheep-stealing case (*Analects* 13.18), which Chapter 9 examined. When family and clan loyalty is not bounded in the public square by an independent scoped procedural symmetry, a society tends toward nepotism and *guanxi*, networks of patronage. Classical mandarin education also put the memorizing of literary classics and ritual form ahead of craftsmanship and experimental science. That order of study let a sophisticated civilization stagnate in technology until Western industrial power confronted it in the nineteenth century.

#### F. Immanuel Kant and David Hume: The Separation of Speculative Philosophy from Practical Reality

Two of the most influential figures of eighteenth-century academic philosophy were David Hume in Scotland and **Immanuel Kant** in Prussia.

**David Hume** was a penetrating historian and economist, and he earned his living by writing. His writings sold so well that he became, in his own words, *"not only independent, but opulent"* (*My Own Life*, 1776). He showed in his *History of England* (six volumes, 1754 to 1762) and in his *Political Discourses* (1752) that he understood property, contract, and the danger of political fanaticism. In his philosophical works, by contrast, his premise that we perceive only disconnected mental impressions led him to claim that we possess no rational justification for believing in cause and effect or the permanence of external objects. He severed speculative philosophy from common-sense reality.

**Immanuel Kant**, prompted by Hume, sought to rescue science and morality by placing external reality beyond human knowledge (*das Ding an sich*, the "thing-in-itself" which Kant claimed we can never know) and relocating order entirely within the structure of the human mind. In ethics, Kant's **Categorical Imperative** (*"Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law"*) captures a valid procedural test ("What if everyone acted on this rule?"). Kant's formulation fails where it detaches procedure from reality. He severed moral duty from keeping and building real human life, from tiered obligation, and from the reality of enemies, reaching a notorious conclusion in his 1797 essay *On a Supposed Right to Lie Because of Philanthropic Concerns*. If a murderer knocks at your door asking whether the friend he is pursuing has taken refuge in your house, Kant argued, you have a categorical duty not to lie to him, even if the truth lets him find and murder your friend.

Under the Five Principles, Kant's answer to the murderer at the door is a failure of **scope** (Principle 4 and Principle 5): a murderer with a weapon in his hand has stepped outside the scope of peaceful civic procedure and made himself an active **enemy**. One owes truth to reality and to peaceful procedural courts; one does not owe assistance to an armed murderer.

#### G. The Modern Collectivist and Anti-Realist Line (Rousseau, Hegel, Marx, Rawls, Foucault, Derrida)

Across two centuries, one modern line of thinkers broke with the Five Principles step by step.

**Jean-Jacques Rousseau** (1712–1778) denied that virtue must be cultivated in human beings (Principle 2), claiming they are naturally good and corrupted only by property and civilization. He attacked keeping and building by calling the first man who fenced a plot of ground to establish a farm an impostor. He also opposed the mediating tiers of family, church, and local guild, demanding in *The Social Contract* that every citizen surrender himself unconditionally to the unitary state's **"General Will"** and be *"forced to be free."* Robespierre and the Jacobins invoked Rousseau's General Will to justify the Terror of 1793–1794.

**G. W. F. Hegel** (1770–1831) replaced correspondence truth with a "dialectical" history in which truth unfolds by historical epoch, and set the state above the household and civil society as "the actuality of the ethical Idea" (*Elements of the Philosophy of Right*, 1820, section 257), against Principle 4. Hegel himself defended private property and the family. What his successors took from him was the historicized truth and the exalted state.

**Karl Marx** (1818–1883) carried Hegel's dialectic into a history in which truth and morality change by class identity. He denied Principle 2 by promising that abolishing private property would remake human nature into the selfless type that later regimes called the "New Socialist Man," and destroyed Principle 5 by replacing symmetrical law with class liquidation. In every instance where Marx's program was implemented in practice, including in Russia, China, Cambodia, Ethiopia, North Korea, and Cuba, it produced the same outcome of tyranny and famine.

In the twentieth century, **John Rawls** (1921–2002), as we saw in Chapter 4 and Chapter 8, used the "Veil of Ignorance" in *A Theory of Justice* (1971) to strip away human particularity. He treated the intergenerational work of parents for their children (Principle 4 and Virtue 7) as an arbitrary "Lottery of Birth" to be equalized away by the state, violating Principle 5.

**Michel Foucault** (1926–1984) and **Jacques Derrida** (1930–2004) completed the repudiation of classical realism by attacking Principle 1 directly, teaching generations of university students that language never refers to objective reality (*"there is nothing outside the text"*) and that truth, reason, science, and law are merely instruments of power (*"regimes of truth"*).

### 2. Specific Religions and Theological Systems Graded by the Five Principles

Religion is one of the deepest forces in human history because it binds together a cosmology (what reality is), an anthropology (what humans are), a moral code (how to live), and an intergenerational community.

#### A. Two-Books Christianity and the Teachings of Jesus

Historically, the mainstream Christian synthesis rested on the **Two-Books Doctrine**. That synthesis built the universities of Bologna, Paris, Oxford, and Cambridge, fostered the Scientific Revolution (Copernicus, Kepler, Galileo, Pascal, Boyle, Newton) and the science that followed it (Faraday, Maxwell, Mendel), and shaped Western constitutional law. It held that a rational Creator authored both the **Book of Scripture** and the **Book of Nature**, so Creation obeys consistent laws rather than capricious magic (Principle 1). It also held that every human being is a moral agent, capable of reason yet prone to sin and self-deception (Principle 2), which explains why unchecked power in human hands seldom stays sound for long.

Even more specifically, the teachings of Jesus of Nazareth in the Gospels are frequently quoted out of scope by both sentimental pacifists and anti-Christian critics. When Jesus's core moral instructions are read within their actual jurisdictional scope, their alignment with the Five Principles is clear.

The first is **the Golden Rule** (Matthew 7:12): *"In everything, treat others as you would want them to treat you, for this fulfills the law and the prophets."* The structure of that precept is direct: before you act toward another person or judge his case, exchange your position with his and ask whether you would accept the same procedure applied to yourself. That is Procedural Symmetry stated in a single sentence that a seven-year-old child can remember for life.

Next is **the beam in your own eye** (Matthew 7:3–5): *"Why do you see the speck in your brother's eye, but fail to see the beam of wood in your own?... First remove the beam from your own eye, and then you can see clearly to remove the speck from your brother's eye."* The order of responsibility is explicit: Self-Anchored, Tiered Ownership (Principle 4 and Virtue 2). Do not begin by policing your neighbor's minor fault while your own household is disordered by a major defect of your own; correct Tier 1 first, *and then* you will possess the clear judgment and moral standing to assist Tier 3.

Third is the pairing of **"Do not judge"** (Matthew 7:1–2) with **"You will recognize them by their fruit"** (Matthew 7:15–20). In modern arguments, whenever one points out that a behavior or ideology is destructive, an opponent frequently quotes the opening words of Matthew 7:1 (*"Do not judge"*) as though Jesus were teaching indiscriminate non-judgmentalism. Read the immediately following sentence (Matthew 7:2): *"For by the standard you judge you will be judged, and the measure you use will be the measure you receive."* And read further on in the same discourse (Matthew 7:15–16): *"Watch out for false prophets, who come to you in sheep's clothing but inwardly are voracious wolves. You will recognize them by their fruit. Grapes are not gathered from thorns or figs from thistles, are they?"* How can anyone distinguish a sheep from a wolf in sheep's clothing, or a fig tree from a thornbush, without exercising **discriminating judgment**? One cannot. Jesus is not forbidding truthful moral and empirical judgment; he is forbidding **hypocritical, asymmetrical judgment** (applying one standard of measure to oneself and a different standard to one's neighbor) and commanding his followers to evaluate teachers and doctrines by their observable consequences in reality (Principles 1, 3, and 5).

Fourth is **"Turn the other cheek"** (Matthew 5:39), which separates personal insult from public defense. When Jesus says in the Sermon on the Mount, *"whoever strikes you on the right cheek, turn the other to him as well,"* the concrete physical detail defines the scope. For a right-handed person facing you to strike your *right* cheek, he must use the **back of his hand**. In first-century Jewish and Roman law, that blow was not a lethal assault, but a formal gesture of **personal insult** intended to provoke a private brawl or duel. Properly scoped, Jesus is teaching personal self-command and refusal to escalate personal insults into private blood feuds. He is *never* commanding a father to stand idle while a murderer kills his children, nor commanding a magistrate or soldier to disband the civil police and armed forces. That is why the New Testament explicitly affirms the tiered duty to provide for and protect one's own family (*"if someone does not provide for his own, especially his own family, he has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever,"* 1 Timothy 5:8, Principle 4) and the magistrate's duty to wield lethal force against criminals and enemies (*"it is God's servant for your well-being... government does not bear the sword for nothing. It is God's servant to administer punishment on the person who does wrong,"* Romans 13:4, Principles 4 and 5).

Two failure modes depart from this classical Two-Books and properly scoped Christian heritage.

The first is Scripturalism (Clark and Crampton). As we examined in depth in Chapter 3, extreme Scripturalism denies the Book of Nature and correspondence truth, claiming that empirical observation can never discover truth and confining inquiry within an unfalsifiable textual system.

The second is progressive church inversion. Across the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, many historic mainline denominations retained their historic stone buildings, stained-glass windows, clerical vestments, and traditional vocabulary on the signboard outside, while completely hollowing out and inverting the internal premises inside. They replaced correspondence truth with therapeutic sentiment, replaced keeping and building family life (Principle 3 and Virtue 6) with ideological activism, erased the distinction between inner-tier duty and borderless universalism, and replaced symmetrical judgment and repentance with unconditional permissiveness. The result, predictably, has been institutional decline within two generations.

#### B. Islam: A Political Law Scoped by the Confession

Islam rests on three sources: the Qur'an, which it holds to be the word of God; the Hadith, the reports of what Muhammad said, did, and approved; and the Sira, the account of his life. The Qur'an binds the three together: *"Whoever obeys the Messenger has obeyed God"* (4:80). A person enters Islam by the confession (*shahada*), *"There is no god but God, and Muhammad is the Messenger of God."* It is the first of the five on which, in the hadith, *"Islam is built"*, followed by prayer, the alms tax (*zakat*), the pilgrimage, and the fast of Ramadan (Bukhari 8). The Qur'an describes itself as sent down from the Lord of the worlds (26:192), free of doubt (2:2), and an explanation of all things (16:89), and it makes judging by what God has sent down a mark of belief (5:44). In the Sira, Muhammad at Medina is prophet, lawgiver, judge, and commander at once, and the Qur'an holds him up as the example to follow (33:21).

Islam is therefore a political order as well as a faith: its law claims the polity. Evaluated against the Five Principles, that order shows three structural conflicts in its foundational texts and legal codes.

**On causation and public truth.** As we saw in Chapter 3, **Al-Ghazali's** Ash'arite occasionalism holds that created things have no action of their own and that the regularity of nature is a habit of God's creating, and al-Azhar's secondary creed textbook still classifies the belief that fire burns by its nature as unbelief. Furthermore, classical jurisprudence developed explicit doctrines of **boundary speech**. *Taqiyya*, developed chiefly in Shi'a jurisprudence as a dispensation under persecution and recognized in Sunni treatises for believers under coercion (on the basis of Qur'an 3:28 and 16:106), permits a believer to conceal his faith or his intentions from outsiders. Where truth-telling is scoped by religious boundary rather than owed symmetrically in the public square, cross-community trust erodes.

Apostasy and blasphemy law also fall under Principle 1, because a revealed claim must still be tested. A man who left Islam met the rule of the hadith *"Whoever changes his religion, kill him"* (Bukhari 6922), which all four Sunni schools and Ja'fari Shi'a law apply; Hanafi and Ja'fari law imprison an apostate woman instead. A Muslim who insults the Prophet is counted an unbeliever and killed in all four schools, and in three of them a non-Muslim who insults him breaks his covenant and loses its protection; the Hanafis dissent. Death for leaving Islam allows the test only one result. Death for insulting the Prophet punishes saying aloud that the claim failed.

**On the scope of the polity and *jizya* (Principles 4 and 5).** Read at its strongest, classical Islamic law is a **scoped procedure**: its rules were set down in advance by the legal schools, administered by trained judges (*qadis*), and applied alike to every person in the same position. The scope it chose is the confession. The jurists divided the world into *Dar al-Islam*, the House of Islam, governed by Islamic law, and *Dar al-Harb*, the House of War, not yet brought under it. Inside *Dar al-Islam*, full symmetry held among Muslims. Jews and Christians who kept their religion lived in a second, lower scope on the terms of **Qur'an 9:29**: *"Fight those who do not believe in God or in the Last Day… among those who were given the Book, until they pay the jizya by hand, brought low."*

The defect is not a lack of procedure but where the scope is drawn. Drawn through one land, it divides neighbors into a ruling tier and a subordinate tier, which Chapter 9 found cannot make one polity.

These rules are not only historical. In Pakistan, insulting the Prophet has carried death and no lesser penalty since a 1991 ruling of the Federal Shariat Court (Penal Code s. 295-C). Its courts sentenced at least eight people to death for blasphemy in 2023, and at least 104 people were killed outside the courts after blasphemy accusations between 1994 and 2024. Pakistan's constitution requires the president to be a Muslim and classes Ahmadis as non-Muslims, and its penal code punishes Ahmadis who present themselves as Muslims (ss. 298-B and 298-C). Iran's penal code sets death for insulting the Prophet (art. 262) and reaches apostasy through the constitution's reference to Islamic sources (art. 220 and constitution art. 167). On 8 May 2023 Iran hanged Yousef Mehrdad and Sadrollah Fazeli Zare for crimes including blasphemy. The same code writes the confession into the law of homicide and sets the measure by religion, the third test of value symmetry in Chapter 9: a Muslim who kills a non-Muslim outside the three recognized religions, such as a Baha'i, faces no retaliation (art. 310), and Iran's civil code bars a non-Muslim from inheriting from a Muslim (art. 881 bis).

Saudi Arabia punishes apostasy with death; Ahmad al-Shamri was sentenced to death for it in 2015, and the Supreme Court upheld the sentence in 2017. Its 2022 Family Law bars a Muslim woman from marrying a non-Muslim man. Mauritania made death mandatory for blasphemy in 2018. Brunei's Syariah Penal Code, in force since 2019, sets death for apostasy and for insulting the Prophet, though an execution moratorium applies. A Sharia court in Kano, Nigeria, sentenced Yahaya Sharif-Aminu to death for blasphemy in 2020, and the Taliban's decree of January 2026 sets death for a Muslim who insults the Prophet and does not repent.

In Egypt, 88 percent of Muslims surveyed by Pew in late 2011 favored the death penalty for those who leave Islam. The same direction appears among Muslims in Western Europe, where no such law applies. In France, an IFOP survey of 1,005 Muslims in 2025 found that 46 percent want Muslim law applied in full or in part, rising to 59 percent of those aged 15 to 24; that 44 percent would follow the rules of their religion over French law on marriage or inheritance; and that 20 percent hold that a Muslim has no right to leave Islam. In Britain, a 2026 survey of 1,006 Muslims for *The Economist* found that 29 percent want sharia to be the main basis of British law or one influence on it; that 20 percent would put Islamic teaching ahead of an act of Parliament, rising to 27 percent of those aged 18 to 24; and that 22 percent hold that violence against a person who insults the Prophet can be justified, rising to 37 percent of those aged 18 to 24.

**On modern revolutionary Islamism (*Velayat-e Faqih* and Muslim Brotherhood doctrine).** In the twentieth century, **Sayyid Qutb** in Egypt and **Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini** in Iran (*Islamic Government: Governance of the Jurist*, 1970) removed even the traditional quietist accommodations of earlier centuries. They turned Islam into a totalizing revolutionary state ideology, rejecting secular constitutional symmetry, national sovereignty, and freedom of conscience as idolatrous (*jahiliyyah* or *taghut*).

A country whose law places every citizen in the same position before it, whatever his religion, draws one scope for everyone inside its borders. A law whose scope is the confession, guarded by *jizya* at its boundary and by death at its exit, cannot share a polity with that rule. Supremacist *sharia* jurisdiction, blasphemy coercion, apostasy penalties, and sectarian legal tiers are incompatible with one law for every citizen, and a country that keeps such a law must grant them no legal jurisdiction within its borders.

#### C. Buddhism: Valuable Tier-1 Mental Discipline, Insufficient Civilizational Root

This assessment covers all three branches of **Buddhism**: Theravada, Mahayana, and Vajrayana.

As a personal psychological and attentional discipline (Tier 1), Buddhism provides genuine value. Its mindfulness, meditation, and analysis of how craving (*tanha*) and undisciplined emotional reactivity cloud human judgment offer practical methods for self-command, composure under pressure, and humility.

Yet Buddhism cannot serve as a self-sufficient root for civilization. Its foundational metaphysical premise is stated in the **Four Noble Truths**: that all conditioned biological and material existence is fundamentally **unsatisfactory suffering (*dukkha*)**, that desire for life and attachment to the world is the cause of that suffering, and that the supreme spiritual goal is **Nirvana**, literally the extinguishing of the flame of desire and the termination of the cycle of biological rebirth (*samsara*). Consequently, the highest ideal of classical Buddhism is the **celibate monk (*bhikkhu*)** who leaves behind wife, children, farm, workshop, and army to live on alms offered by lay households.

Judged on its own terms, an ideal that is the root of a civilization has to be able to sustain itself when people actually attain it. If every member attains the highest Buddhist ideal of celibate monastic non-attachment, no one cultivates the fields, no one manufactures tools, no one defends the frontier, and no children are born. The society ends within one generation. Continuance then depends on people who do not attain the ideal, or who set it aside. Jain mendicants meet the same test: the highest form does not pick the fruit or till the soil, and lay households do that work so the mendicant can keep the rule. A root that survives only by being broken is not a root.

#### D. Shinto: Strong on Continuity and Beauty, Vulnerable When Bound to Blood Mysticism

Shinto aligns strongly with several of the virtues. It fosters a strong cultural ethic of physical cleanliness, care for tools and craft (Virtues 2 and 3), reverence for natural and architectural beauty, and deep generational gratitude connecting living households to their ancestors (*kami*) and descendants, the living heeding what their elders handed down and forming those who come after. In a Japanese workshop or train station, the practical results of those habits are evident.

Its vulnerability appeared when it was detached from Principle 1 and Principle 5. Traditional Shinto is a particularist folk religion without a universal doctrine of correspondence truth or universal human nature. Between 1890 and 1945, the Meiji and Showa militarists fused it into **State Shinto**, teaching as state dogma that the Japanese Emperor was a living descendant of the sun goddess and that non-Japanese peoples occupied an inferior ontological tier. That state creed silenced internal rational dissent (violating Principle 1) and licensed atrocities against conquered neighbors (violating Principle 5), culminating in the catastrophe of 1945.

### 3. Right-Wing Anti-Proceduralists and Surface-Aligned False Friends, Including Alexander Dugin

The same evaluative criteria apply to an error that frequently entraps observers who recognize the defects of modern left-wing progressivism.

When an intelligent young man or woman realizes that postmodern constructivism, forced leveling of outcomes under "equity," open borders, and family breakdown are destroying their country, their first impulse is often to embrace any writer who loudly denounces modern liberalism.

**The foundational premises of such doctrines must be tested before they are accepted.**

**The enemy of a bad idea is not automatically a good idea.** If a house has termites in the floorboards, setting the house on fire will certainly destroy the termites, yet it will also destroy the house and its inhabitants.

In recent years, two clusters of "anti-liberal" thinkers on the political right have gained followings by attacking progressive decay. Both would set aside procedural symmetry or the checks that bind rulers. Alexander Dugin, the theorist of the second cluster, attacks universal truth and keeping and building as well.

#### A. Western Right-Wing Anti-Proceduralists (Wolfe, Yarvin, Haywood)

Three contemporary Western writers urge conservatives toward rule by one man or by one confession.

**Stephen Wolfe** (*The Case for Christian Nationalism*, 2022) rightly notes that human beings are particular and need cultural continuity (Principle 4). He then proposes a Christian prince, envisioning "a measured and theocratic Caesarism." He writes that the civil magistrate "can punish external religion," naming "heretical teaching, false rites, blasphemy, and Sabbath-breaking," though not belief itself. Non-Christians, he writes, "are entitled to justice, peace, and safety, but they are not entitled to political equality." In a 2023 interview he held it "permissible in principle to execute arch-heretics." A magistrate who holds the sword against heretical teaching must decide which teaching is heretical, and under Principle 2 he decides as a limited and self-interested man. European history from 1524 to 1648 shows where that leads. The Thirty Years' War (1618 to 1648) alone cost the Holy Roman Empire about five million lives, counting deaths from disease, a fifth of its population before the war (Peter H. Wilson, *The Thirty Years War: Europe's Tragedy*, 2009). That history is why American Christians established the constitutional separation of church and state. A magistrate given the sword to enforce theology corrupts the church and persecutes his fellow Christians over which confession is orthodox.

**Curtis Yarvin** (*Patchwork*, 2008, and later essays) rightly diagnoses how unaccountable administrative bureaucracies and media-academic complexes ("the Cathedral") subvert democratic accountability. His remedy is a realm "governed by its own joint-stock corporation without regard to the residents' opinions." He calls himself "an absolute monarchist," calls the separation of powers a "bogus political panacea," and holds that "no sovereign can be subject to law." In 2024 he wrote that an American executive will not work until the President "considers both the judicial and legislative branches purely ceremonial and advisory." He grants the danger that Principle 2 names: "even the best king can develop a brain tumor, go insane, and order the extermination of the Irish." His answer is that the shareholders may dismiss the chief executive. That check is held by the owners, whose interest is the return on the realm. The residents, who bear the ruler's errors, hold no check at all: "The design is all 'exit,' no 'voice.'" Under Principle 2 the owners are as limited and self-interested as the ruler they appoint.

**Charles Haywood** ("The Foundationalist Manifesto," 2021) proposes a government that "will not be democratic." It "could be Augustan," he writes, "a limited dictatorship (and almost certainly will be to begin)," and "In pursuit of its limited ends, it will have unlimited means." He looks for a Caesar, "a sovereign dictator, who channels the constituent power of the nation to create something that did not exist before, with a fresh constitution." Haywood praises the rule of law elsewhere, but he expects that under Caesar "the rule of law is likely to bend, if not break at some places and times," and that Caesar "will, at least sometimes, therefore resort to proscription and extra-legal action." His advice for the day Caesar comes is to "celebrate." Proscription is a penalty decided after the fact against named persons. It fails Principle 5, which requires rules known beforehand and applied alike. Under Principle 2, the man trusted to bend the law for a good end is limited and self-interested, and once the law bends, the citizens have no procedure to stop him when his ends change.

#### B. Alexander Dugin and *The Fourth Political Theory*: The Collectivist Leveler in Traditionalist Robes

The most influential global theorist of this anti-Western, anti-procedural right is the Russian philosopher **Alexander Dugin** (born 1962), author of *The Foundations of Geopolitics* (1997) and *The Fourth Political Theory* (2009).

Because Dugin borrows the language of conservatism, praising "Tradition," the Orthodox Church, multipolarity, and resistance to Western progressive decadence, some Western right-wing commentators have naively treated him as a civilizational ally.

Dugin's actual writings and speeches state his core premises directly, allowing them to be evaluated against the Five Common Principles of Reality.

First is Dugin's explicit hostility to individual success and excellence (Principle 2, Principle 3, and Virtue 3). In a post on X on 10 July 2026, he stated his moral premise toward individual human achievement without ambiguity:

> *"The very concept of the success is deeply immoral... We should prosper or suffer together. Otherwise it is satanic."*

According to Dugin, an industrious farmer's success is "deeply immoral" and "satanic" unless he suffers in poverty alongside his neighbor. One farmer rises at five in the morning, clears the weeds from his field, repairs his roof, remains sober, and harvests an abundant crop. His neighbor sleeps until noon, drinks heavily, and lets his roof decay. That is the destructive pathology of the old Russian peasant commune (*mir*) and Soviet collectivization. It is the envious leveling mentality illustrated in the old Russian proverb in which a peasant, offered any wish on condition that his neighbor receive double, asks to have one of his own eyes put out. There is a direct structural parallel between **radical outcome-egalitarianism** in the West and **Dugin's Eurasianist Collectivism** in the East. Both look upon a human being who achieves excellence and builds a flourishing life. Both declare that unequal achievement is evil and that all persons must be compelled to *"suffer together"* in shared deprivation.

Second, Dugin denies universal correspondence truth. In *The Fourth Political Theory*, he explicitly rejects the existence of universal objective truth, universal natural science, and universal human nature. Drawing directly on **Martin Heidegger** and **postmodern constructivism**, Dugin declares that what the West calls empirical truth and logic is merely a local "Atlanticist / Anglo-Saxon episteme," and that Russia/Eurasia possesses its own separate "archaic truth" in which state myth overrides factual correspondence. Under Principle 1, truth has one frame, which is reality, and a state myth is a narrative. That is postmodern constructivism restated in the vocabulary of Russian imperial nationalism.

Third, Dugin erases the individual and the self-anchored household into *Dasein* (Principles 2 and 4). He explicitly names the **individual human person** as the primary enemy to be abolished. The First Political Theory (liberalism) took the individual as its subject. The Second (communism) took class, and the Third (fascism) took race or state. Dugin proposes that his "Fourth Political Theory" takes Heidegger's collective *Dasein* ("being-there") and the undifferentiated mass (*Narod*) as the only real actor. He declares explicitly that the individual possesses no intrinsic boundary, no inviolable property, and no self-anchored standing apart from the collective empire.

Fourth, Dugin abolishes procedural symmetry in favor of eternal imperial war. He rejects legal symmetry, individual property rights, and constitutional checks inside the state, and he views international politics as a permanent war between "Land Power" (Eurasia) and "Sea Power" (the constitutional West). His system therefore cannot build high-trust domestic courts or peaceful prosperity. Instead, the system requires permanent external conquest and internal secret-police mobilization to justify domestic poverty and coercion.

The verdict under the Five Principles is clear: Alexander Dugin is not a defender of civilization against postmodernism; he is a collectivist leveler and postmodern anti-realist whose program leads to serfdom and imperial warfare.
