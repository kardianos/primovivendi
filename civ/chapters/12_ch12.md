### 1. Specific Philosophers and Philosophical Schools Graded by the Five Principles

#### A. Scottish Common Sense Realism (Thomas Reid, James Beattie, James Oswald)

As we saw in Chapter 2, the eighteenth-century Scottish Common Sense Realists—led by **Thomas Reid** (*An Inquiry into the Human Mind on the Principles of Common Sense*, 1764), alongside **James Beattie** and **James Oswald**—provide one of the cleanest philosophical articulations of **Principle 1** and **Principle 2** in Western thought.

* **Where they excel:** Reid saw clearly that when indoor philosophers like René Descartes, John Locke (in his theory of "ideas" as mental screens), Bishop Berkeley, and **David Hume** claimed that humans never perceive the real world directly and cannot know causation or external reality, those philosophers were sawing off the branch all human inquiry sits on. Reid proved that every skeptic secretly relies on the reliability of his senses, memory, and reason the moment he steps out of his study to cross the street or eat dinner. Furthermore, Reid grounded active moral agency (Will) and practical duty in the real constitution of human nature.
* **Civilizational Legacy:** It is no accident that Scottish Common Sense Realism became the backbone curriculum of early American universities (such as Princeton under John Witherspoon, who taught James Madison) during the exact decades when the American constitutional republic and the Industrial Revolution were being built.

#### B. Classical Constitutionalism (John Locke, James Madison, James Wilson, Thomas Jefferson)

The architects of classical Anglo-American constitutionalism combined Scottish Realism and English common-law procedure to solve the hardest problem of **Principle 2** and **Principle 5**: how to build a strong government run by flawed, self-interested mortals without letting the rulers plunder the citizens.

* **John Locke** (*Second Treatise of Government*, 1689) rightly recognized that life, liberty, and property (the fruit of a person's labor mixed with the earth) exist prior to the state, and that people form governments to act as an impartial procedural umpire (**Principle 5**) rather than remaining in a state of private blood feuds. (Where Locke was weak—his *tabula rasa* or "blank slate" metaphor in epistemology and his "idea-screen" theory of perception—Thomas Reid and **James Wilson**, one of the primary drafters of the U.S. Constitution and a fellow Scot trained in Common Sense Realism, supplied the needed corrective.)
* **James Madison** (*Federalist* Nos. 10 and 51) is the supreme political engineer of **Principle 2**: instead of daydreaming that elections would fill Congress with selfless angels, Madison designed separated branches, bicameral checks, federalism, and enumerated scopes of jurisdiction so that *"ambition must be made to counteract ambition."*

#### C. Burkean Conservatism (Edmund Burke, Russell Kirk)

As we saw in Chapter 10, **Edmund Burke** (and twentieth-century successors such as **Russell Kirk**) articulated the core truths of **Principle 2** (humility before human cognitive limits), **Principle 3** (how hard it is to build an institution and how easy to burn it down), **Principle 4** (that love of country begins in what Burke called the *"little platoons"* of family and neighborhood), and **Virtue 7** (the intergenerational covenant between the dead, the living, and the unborn).

* **The Necessary Engineering Pairing:** Burkean respect for inherited custom must always remain anchored to **Principle 1** (objective correspondence truth) and **Principle 3** (building as well as keeping). If "tradition" is detached from reality testing, a society can end up embalming broken habits like a museum curator instead of repairing and advancing its tools.

#### D. Classical Stoicism (Hierocles, Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius)

* **Where Stoicism aligns:** The Roman Stoics excelled at **Tier 1 self-command and Virtue 2 (Ownership)**. **Epictetus**, born a lame slave in Hierapolis, taught with unforgettable clarity the distinction between what is up to us (our own judgments, integrity, and actions) and what is not up to us—forbidding the excuse-making of external blame. **Hierocles** mapped the concentric circles of tiered obligation (**Principle 4**), and the Emperor **Marcus Aurelius** (*Meditations*) modeled tireless outward civic and military duty on the Danubian frontier when he would rather have been reading philosophy at home.
* **Where Stoicism requires Principle 3:** When Stoic detachment (*apatheia*) is pushed too far into fatalistic resignation—treating the external material world as completely indifferent—it can dull the drive to **build** new technology and transform physical reality for future generations. Stoic self-command is a magnificent rudder, provided **Principle 3** supplies the wind in the sails.

#### E. Confucianism (Confucius / Kongzi, Mencius)

* **Where Confucianism aligns:** Across more than two millennia in East Asia, Confucianism proved extraordinarily durable at reinforcing **Tier 2 and Tier 3 family stability**, filial obligation across generations (**Virtue 7**), disciplined study (**Virtue 3**), and polite social order.
* **Where Confucianism is vulnerable:** As we saw in the Duke of She's sheep-stealing case (*Analects* 13.18), when family and clan loyalty is not bounded by an independent **Principle 5 (Scoped Procedural Symmetry)** in the public square, a society slides toward nepotism and guanxi (crony networks). Furthermore, when classical mandarinate education prioritized memorizing literary classics and ritual form over empirical craftsmanship and experimental science, it allowed a brilliant civilization to stagnate technologically until jolted by Western industrial power in the nineteenth century.

#### F. Immanuel Kant and David Hume: The Brilliant Indoor Splits

Look at the two towering figures of eighteenth-century academic philosophy—**David Hume** in Scotland and **Immanuel Kant** in Prussia:

* **David Hume** was a penetrating historian and economist outside his study—understanding property, contract, and the danger of political fanaticism—yet inside his philosophical treatise, his premise that we perceive only disconnected mental impressions led him to claim that we have zero rational justification for believing in cause and effect or the permanence of objects. He split philosophy from common-sense reality (**Principle 1**).
* **Immanuel Kant**, awakened by Hume, tried to rescue science and morality by locking reality in an unreachable vault (*das Ding an sich*, the "thing-in-itself" which Kant claimed we can never know) and relocating order entirely inside the structure of the human mind. In ethics, Kant's **Categorical Imperative** (*"Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law"*) captures a valid procedural test ("What if everyone did this?"). **Wait and see where Kant fails:** because Kant severed moral duty from **Principle 3** (keeping and building real human life) and **Principle 4** (tiered scope, recognizing that enemies are real), he famously argued in his 1797 essay *On a Supposed Right to Lie Because of Philanthropic Concerns* that if a murderer knocks on your door asking whether your innocent friend or child is hiding inside your house, you have a categorical duty to tell the murderer the exact truth so he can walk in and slit their throats!
* Under the Five Principles, Kant's answer to the murderer at the door is an absurd failure of **scope** (**Principle 4 and Principle 5**): a murderer with an axe in his hand has stepped outside the scope of peaceful civic procedure and made himself an active **enemy**. You owe truth to reality and to peaceful procedural courts; you do not owe target coordinates to an invading killer!

    *"Therefore all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them: for this is the law and the prophets."*

    Look at the structure of that sentence: before you act toward another person or judge his case, **swap your label with his label and ask whether you would accept the exact same procedure applied to yourself.** That is **Procedural Symmetry (Principle 5)** stated in a single sentence that a seven-year-old child can remember for life.
