## The axioms

This book answers the five principal questions; other answers exist and appear later. The first concerns the world, and when a claim may be called true. The second names the human who must judge both: limited, particular, and self-interested. The third declares what the good is. The fourth and fifth set duty and justice under that good.

**Reality is real, knowable, and consequential.** We live in a reality that exists whether any man likes it, names it, or votes upon it. That reality can be known, not perfectly but with usable confidence; and its facts carry consequences which no declaration can set aside. Bodies need food, shelter, and heat; those needs are met or not met by what in fact happens, never by what is declared. Truth is correspondence to this reality: a claim is true when the claim matches what is. Men must plant, build, heal, and defend together. Shared work needs shared knowledge, and shared knowledge needs a test no party may rewrite for its own cause. Correspondence is that test: does the claim track the world? Our models of the world are provisional, and wherever a model can be brought to trial, the world is the judge of it. A claim that can be tested must be stated with precision enough for the trial, and must be tested as it was advanced, not rewritten after the outcome so that it always appears to have won. When a model and the world disagree upon a matter which the world can decide, the model yields. Even a model that predicts well can carry a wrong or an incomplete story inside it, and a lucky hit under bad grounds is still a poor map. The next section takes up how claims are graded and how credit is awarded.

The test is physical. A village may vote that the old bridge is sound: the vote does not pull the rot from the timbers, and the cart rolls onto the span to be carried across or to be dropped into the river. A harvest comes in or fails, whatever was said at the planting feast. A knife cuts the rope or does not, whatever the seller promised. The vote, the feast, and the promise can each move men to act; not one of them can alter the timber, the grain, or the edge. Public life must treat the world as independent of wish.

**Man is limited, particular, and self-interested.** He is limited, finite in time, energy, knowledge, and reach, and finite in what he can be made into. No social force turns him into another kind of creature while he remains a man who means to live. He is particular, this person, of a family and a chain that formed him, in a situation that is his. He is self-interested, caring for his own life and well-being, and for life continuing in those who are his and in what he hands on, not first for mankind as a crowd without faces or households. That care is good. Alone it is not yet life among many under shared rules; it remains the real motive of a living man, and it only ever runs through someone in particular. He is not a blank slate for total redesign by society or power, nor is he naturally good and perfectible by remaking the whole order. Virtue is trained and kept by parents, by teachers, and by the person who practices it. The axioms are for that creature, and not for an interchangeable token.

**The good includes life and thriving.** So far the answers have said what is. An ought must now be chosen. The good this book affirms includes, at minimum, human life and human thriving: survival first, then order, skill, health, building, and transmission past bare continuation. This answer is an axiom: it is chosen, not deduced. No chain of pure description can compel a man to value life; this book therefore states the value openly and gives its reasons. Without living persons, no value, justice, beauty, or kindness has a holder. Every living person stands at the end of a chain of parents who continued life.

Under this axiom three things stay distinct: the axiom itself, its corollaries, and the conduct it asks of us. The axiom: life continuing, and continuing well, is good. The corollaries: institutions must be able to persist; thriving is required for survival, not merely added to it; and the chain of parents is honored by transmitting language, skill, law, and formation, not by living upon the work of parents while despising that work. This is not a claim that every culture should continue. A culture may be judged, and found worthy of continuance or unworthy of it; what cannot be judged away is the requirement that a people thrive if it would survive at all. If a tribe wallows in the mud, let no one be surprised when an iron-age civilization rolls over it without a thought. The conduct: to bear children, or to take a real part in raising them, and to order life so that continuation is honorable and common. This conduct is a duty and an honor because the axiom is affirmed; it is not deduced from the bare fact that one exists, and it does not conscript every person into the same office.

**Priority runs from the self outward.** Human time, energy, knowledge, and focus are limited. What is given to one claim cannot at the same moment be given to another. Each person must therefore order the claims upon him, beginning with himself and moving outward. Each person stands nearest to himself: he must keep his own means in order before he can be of use to any other, and a parent knows the child's cough better than a stranger's need upon another continent. This is not a license to care for no one beyond the door; it is the recognition that positions differ in fact. The scope of a parent with a child is not the scope of a teacher with a student, nor of the experienced with the inexperienced, and each position carries its own claim. From each person the claims run outward: the household, then family, neighbors, community, polity, and the wider world, real at every distance but weaker as the distance grows. A man who declares unlimited duty ends by performing none. Ranking is not hatred of strangers: no duty vanishes past the door, and the nation is not the only real unit by metaphysics. What this answer works through are scopes: a role, a membership, a jurisdiction in which obligation can actually be discharged. Borders, household authority, and professional roles are scopes of that kind.

**Justice is procedural symmetry.** The same procedure is applied to every person who stands in the same position. In inquiry, one critical standard for every claim, so that truth may be sought rather than decided in advance. In law, one rule for the same case, whoever stands before the court. In conduct, the test of reversal: would you still accept the rule if the positions were exchanged? Between the generations, the treatment you wish had been used toward you: life given, formation given, a platform to stand on. Symmetry of procedure does not deny the real differences of position named under the preceding answer; it governs within them, so that two persons who occupy the same station are judged by the same measure. Limited and self-interested men must cooperate; cooperation requires stable expectations; and stable expectations require rules which do not change with the person. Justice is not equal end-states for everyone; nor is it equal return in kind. What this answer rejects is one-way rules for favored groups, and a mercy that never names the wrong, which is not mercy but permissiveness.

**The answers stand together or not at all.** Truth pursued without life as the good dwindles into trivia, or is made an instrument of death. Life held as the good without truth mistakes wishes for facts, and policy is then built upon wishes. Priority without procedure becomes the favoritism of the clan and exemption for one's own. Procedure without priority becomes a universal claim which no limited creature can keep. Each answer without the rest would be a different and lesser thing. Taken together, the five are the position of this book.

**Charge.** These are the answers held here: reality is real, knowable, and consequential, and truth is correspondence to it; man is limited, particular, and self-interested, not a blank slate and not naturally good or perfectible by remaking the whole order; the good includes life and thriving; priority runs from the self outward; justice is the same procedure for every person in the same position, not equal end-states and not equal return in kind. Next comes how to look at what can be tested: which claims reality will judge, and how to let reality judge them.
