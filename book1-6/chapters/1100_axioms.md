## The axioms

This book answers the five principal questions, and it answers them before it argues with anyone, for a position ought to be shown whole before it is set against any other. Other answers exist. They will have their turn.

**Reality is real, knowable, and consequential.** We live in a reality that exists whether any man likes it, names it, or votes upon it. It can be known; not perfectly, and never finally, but with usable confidence, and usable confidence is enough to plant by and to build by. It is consequential: bodies need food, shelter, and heat, and whether the needs are met turns on what in fact happens; no declaration ever fed a child or sealed a roof. Truth is correspondence to this reality: a claim is true when it matches what is. The definition looks like a small thing, and it is not, for men must plant and build and heal and defend together, and they cannot work together unless what is true for one is true for the other. Shared work needs shared knowledge; shared knowledge needs a test that no party may rewrite for its own cause; correspondence is that test. Every model we hold is provisional. Wherever a model can be brought to trial, the world is the judge of it, and the trial has terms: the claim must be stated with precision enough to be tried, and tried as it was advanced, not rewritten after the outcome so that it always appears to have won. When the model and the world disagree on a matter the world can decide, the model yields. A model that predicts well may still carry a wrong or an incomplete story inside it, for a lucky hit under bad grounds is still a poor map.

The test is physical. A village may vote that the old bridge is sound. The vote does not pull the rot out of the timbers; the cart that rolls onto the span is carried across or dropped into the river by the state of the beams, not by the count of the hands. A harvest comes in or it fails, whatever was said at the planting feast. A knife cuts the rope or it does not, whatever the seller promised. Vote, feast, and promise move men, and that is all they move. Public life must treat the world as independent of wish. The grading of claims, and the giving of credit for them, belong to the next section.

**Man is limited, particular, and self-interested.** He is limited, finite in time, energy, knowledge, and reach, and finite in what he can be made into. No social force turns him into another kind of creature while he remains a man who means to live. He is particular, this person, of a family and a chain that formed him, in a situation that is his. He is self-interested, caring for his own life and well-being, and for life continuing in those who are his and in what he hands on, not first for mankind as a crowd without faces or households. That care is good. Alone it is not yet life among many under shared rules; it remains the real motive of a living man, and it only ever runs through someone in particular. He is not a blank slate for total redesign by society or power, nor is he naturally good and perfectible by remaking the whole order. Virtue is trained and kept by parents, by teachers, and by the man who practices it, and it is trained in someone in particular or it is not trained at all. The axioms are for that creature, not for an interchangeable man; and it is that creature, limited and often wrong, who must judge what is true and what is good.

**The good includes life and thriving.** The answers so far have said what is; they have not said what ought to be. No chain of pure description can compel a man to value life, so the ought cannot be deduced; it must be chosen, and this book chooses it in the open. The good affirmed here includes, at minimum, human life and human thriving: survival first, then order, skill, health, building, and transmission past bare continuation. That is the axiom, and two reasons are given with it. Without living persons nothing is valued and nothing is just, nothing is beautiful and nothing is kind, for there is no one left to hold any of it. And every living person stands at the end of a chain of parents who continued life; that choice was made in his favor before he could weigh it, or he would not be here to weigh anything.

Three things must be kept apart here: the axiom, its corollaries, and the conduct it asks. The axiom is that life continuing, and continuing well, is good. The corollaries follow close behind: institutions must be able to persist; thriving is required for survival, not merely added to it; and the chain of parents is honored by handing on language, skill, law, and formation, not by living on the work of parents while despising that work. No part of this says that every culture must continue. Cultures can be judged. A culture may be found worthy to continue, or unworthy; what cannot be judged away is that a people must thrive if it would survive at all. If a tribe wallows in the mud, let no one be surprised when an iron-age civilization rolls over it without a thought. The conduct asked is plain: bear children, or take a real part in raising them, and order life so that continuing is honorable and common. That conduct is a duty and an honor because the axiom is affirmed, not a deduction from the bare fact that one exists; and it does not conscript every person into the same office.

**Priority runs from the self outward.** A man's time is limited, and so are his energy, his knowledge, and his focus; what he spends on one claim he cannot spend on another at the same hour. So the claims on him must be ranked, and the ranking begins with himself: he must keep his own means in order before he can be of use to any other, and a parent knows the cough of the child in the next room better than the need of a stranger on another continent. This is not leave to care for no one beyond his own door. Positions differ, and the difference is real: the scope of a parent with a child is not the scope of a teacher with a student, nor of the experienced with the inexperienced, and each position carries its own claim. From the self the claims run outward, through household and family and neighbors, through community and polity, out to the wider world; every one of them is real, and every one is weaker than the one before. The man who declares a duty to everyone ends by performing a duty to no one. Nor is the ranking hatred of the stranger; no duty vanishes with distance, and the nation is not the only real thing between a man and mankind. The ranking works through scopes: a role, a membership, a jurisdiction inside which an obligation can actually be discharged. A border is such a scope; so is the authority of a household; so is a trade.

**Justice is procedural symmetry.** The same procedure holds for every person in the same position, whoever he is and whoever he knows. In inquiry that means one critical standard for every claim, so that truth may be sought rather than decided in advance; in law it means one rule for the same case, whoever stands before the court. In conduct it is the old test of reversal: would you still accept the rule if the positions were exchanged? Between the generations it is the treatment you wish had been used toward you: life given, formation given, a platform under your feet. None of this denies that positions really differ; the last answer said so, and symmetry governs inside those differences, so that two persons in the same station are judged by one measure. And symmetry is not a nicety. Limited and self-interested men must cooperate to live; cooperation needs expectations that hold; and expectations hold only under rules that do not bend to the person. Justice is not equal end-states, and it is not equal return in kind. A rule that runs one way for a favored group is not justice at all; and a mercy that never names the wrong is not mercy, but permissiveness.

**The answers stand together or not at all.** Truth pursued without life as the good dwindles into trivia, or is made an instrument of death. Life held as the good without truth mistakes wishes for facts and builds policy on the wishes. Priority without procedure decays into favoritism of the clan and exemption for one's own. Procedure without priority lays on a limited creature a claim without limit, and he keeps none of it. Cut any one of the five away from the rest, and what is left is a different and lesser thing. Together they are the position of this book.

**Charge.** These, then, are the answers held here. Reality is real, knowable, and consequential, and truth is correspondence to it. Man is limited, particular, and self-interested; he is no blank slate, he is not naturally good, and no remaking of the whole order will perfect him. The good includes life and thriving. Priority runs from the self outward. Justice is one procedure for every person in the same position, not equal end-states, not equal return in kind. Next comes how to look at what can be tested: which claims reality will judge, and how to let reality judge them.
