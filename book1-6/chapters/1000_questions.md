# Foundations

## The five questions

Societies argue about policies; those fights fail while the premises stay hidden. The deeper division sits beneath the arguments, in a small set of questions which few men ever pronounce aloud, and it is with these questions that this book begins. Two citizens may attend the same church and vote for the same party, and yet return opposite answers upon the nature of truth, the nature of man, and the demands of justice. The shared label conceals the division until some hard case (an inheritance, a hiring decision, a war) compels the answers into the open.

<!-- arc: Q6 | point: unspoken answers already govern conduct; actions reveal them -->
Whether or not a person states answers, that person's actions already contain them. A father who pays for his own daughter's schooling before funding any distant cause has answered the question of priority. A voter who reads unequal pay as automatic injustice has answered the question of justice. A colleague who shrugs "true for you" at a factual dispute has answered the question of truth. A couple that treats children as an optional luxury, and a people that treats raising children as its main work, have each answered the question of the good. The only choice on offer is between answers examined and answers absorbed without notice.

The five questions are these:

1. What is truth? Is reality objectively knowable? Can we know reality with usable confidence? Is knowledge mainly discovery that answers to the world, or only a product of power, will, or pure dialectic? What makes a claim true?

2. What is a human being by nature? Are human beings limited and self-interested; blank slates waiting to be written; naturally good, perfectible, and restorable by remaking the whole social (or sacral-political) order; or interchangeable units for whom any station and any chain of birth would do the same moral work?

3. What is the standard of the good: what ought human life and society aim at?

4. When a man cannot give himself fully to every claim at once, and the claims on him differ in urgency and nearness, what should govern how he spends himself?

5. What does justice require: equal procedures within a scope, equal outcomes for everyone, or equal value returned in kind?

An easy way to remember the five questions is this: reality; the self in reality; the self among others. The first two are descriptive, and ask what the world is and what the creature who dwells in it is. The third turns from what is to what ought to be, and it is put without naming any standard of the good, lest the question should presume its own answer. The fourth and fifth ask how a man spends himself when claims conflict, and what justice requires, under whatever standard the third shall establish, given what the first two have said of the world and of the creature.

These questions are practical: they foretell who can cooperate and who cannot. When a decision begins to cost something, observe who in fact stands with you. The divisions that matter run along the answers to these five questions, not along the labels on the church door or the party card. A neighbor who shares your answers upon truth, upon human nature, upon the good, upon priority, and upon justice can plan with you across a decade; a colleague who differs on all five cannot, whatever organizations you both belong to.

Then write down what you believe about the world, about yourself, and about society. Ask these five questions of yourself, and prefer them to any label which you or any other man may wear. Do not proceed upon assumptions you have never examined: weigh your present convictions, and consider whether the answers this book is about to propose may serve as a good beginning. The next chapter states those answers as axioms; the remainder of the book treats of how to live under them.
